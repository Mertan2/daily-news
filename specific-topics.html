💴 The yen paradox: a rate hike, and a 23-month low

the setupTextbook says raising rates strengthens a currency. This week Japan did the opposite of what the textbook predicts: the BOJ hiked to 1.0% on Monday, and by Thursday the yen had fallen to ¥160.79 — its weakest in about 23 months, past the record set in April. Understanding why is the cleanest window into the whole global rate picture.

It's the gap, not the level

  • Currencies trade on rate differentials, not absolute rates. Japan moving to 1.0% matters only relative to where everyone else sits — and the US sits at 3.50–3.75% with a Fed now leaning toward a hike.
  • So even after the BOJ tightened, the US–Japan gap stayed near ~2.75 points and looks set to widen, not narrow. Capital keeps flowing to the higher-yielding dollar.
  • The hike was also ~90% priced in before it happened, so it delivered no surprise to buy the yen on — while the BOJ's "no rush" guidance signaled the gap won't close fast.

The carry trade, reignited

  • The mechanism is the yen carry trade: borrow cheaply in yen, convert to dollars, earn the spread. Every turn of it sells yen — and a hawkish Warsh Fed just made the spread more attractive.
  • That is why a tightening BOJ and a falling yen coexist: monetary policy and FX flows are pulling in opposite directions.
  • The same weak yen lit up exporters — the Nikkei broke 71,000 for the first time — so Tokyo faces the awkward mix of record equities and an uncomfortably weak currency.
Why it matters: ¥161 is the zone where Japan has intervened before, and Chief Cabinet Secretary Kihara already warned of "appropriate action…at any time." Watch for actual MOF intervention, any verbal escalation from the BOJ toward a faster path, and the risk that a sudden carry-trade unwind — if the Fed ever does pivot — snaps the move back violently, as it did in 2024.

🛢️ The $24bn question inside the Iran deal

where it standsWith the 14-point "Islamabad memorandum" now official and the ceremony set for Friday at the Bürgenstock resort, the most revealing detail wasn't the diplomacy — it was Trump's blunt admission about the money. The deal releases ~$24bn in frozen Iranian assets over the 60-day nuclear-talks window, and the president framed returning it not as a concession but as a necessity for the dollar.

Why "we have to give it back"

  • Trump said the US had taken "a lot" of Iran's money and "we're going to have to give it back," tying repayment explicitly to maintaining global confidence in the dollar.
  • The logic: if Washington is seen seizing and keeping sovereign reserves at will, other states accelerate diversifying away from dollar assets — the long-run cost of weaponized finance.
  • It is a striking reversal of posture, and a tell that the administration sees the reserve-currency franchise as a strategic asset worth more than the cash itself.

What's locked vs. what's still open

  • Locked in practice: end of hostilities "on all fronts including Lebanon," blockade lifted within 30 days, Hormuz reopened, oil-sanctions suspension, the asset release schedule.
  • Still open: the actual nuclear file (enrichment, stockpiles), the comprehensive sanctions architecture, and the disputed Lebanon clause — all pushed into the 60-day track. Hamas's reported "broad consensus" on Gaza ceasefire phase two and a fresh UN child-protection listing of Israeli forces keep the Levant volatile.
The cleanest live gauge is still oil, not communiqués: Brent toward $78 says markets believe the guns are quiet. Watch whether the asset tranches actually move on schedule, whether the Lebanon language survives Israeli operations, and the quieter signal — whether sovereign reserve managers read the repayment as a genuine guardrail on US financial coercion.

🤖 The race to the first near-trillion-dollar AI IPO

the setupSpaceX's record listing cracked the IPO window open; now the two frontier AI labs are sprinting through it. Both filed confidential S-1s within a week of each other, both are chasing near-$1 trillion valuations, and the order they price in could reset the AI pecking order.

The two filers

  • Anthropic — filed first, June 1. Valued around $965bn after a late-May raise, on a reported ~$47bn annualized run-rate from Claude. Widely placed on the faster track, with pricing possible as early as October.
  • OpenAI — confirmed June 8 it had also submitted a confidential S-1, with annualized recurring revenue reported above $20bn. Larger mindshare, but seen a step behind on the listing calendar.

Why the sequence matters

  • At ~$1tn, the first to price would vault straight into the top tier of the S&P 500, alongside a handful of mega-caps — an index event, not just an IPO.
  • Going first sets the valuation comp the other is measured against, and captures the scarce pool of investors wanting concentrated AI exposure.
  • The listings monetize the compute-and-capital flywheel: Nvidia's "Vera" CPUs and "RTX Spark" chips name both labs as early adopters, so IPO proceeds largely recycle back into the silicon that powers them.
Why it matters: a successful near-trillion-dollar AI listing would be the clearest market verdict yet on whether frontier-model revenue justifies frontier-model valuations — and it lands precisely as a hawkish Fed reprices long-duration tech. Watch the pricing window (the autumn slot Anthropic is reportedly targeting), the first disclosed full financials, and whether a wobble in either book cools the broader AI-IPO supercycle.
