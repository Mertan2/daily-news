🏦 Three central banks, three directions, three days

the setupIt is rare for the Fed, the BOJ and the ECB to all decide inside a 48-hour window — and rarer still for them to move three different ways. This week they did, each reacting to the same Middle East energy shock from a different starting point.

The Fed — held, but hawkish (June 16–17)

  • Rate held at 3.50–3.75% — a fourth straight hold, and the first decision under new chair Kevin Warsh.
  • The jolt was in the projections: the dot plot dropped the last 2026 cut, with some members now sketching a hike, after May CPI hit 4.2%.
  • Warsh, a longstanding dot-plot skeptic, leaned toward less detailed forward guidance — a structural shift markets read as repricing risk for long-duration tech and AI equities.

The BOJ — hiked to a 30-year high (June 16)

  • Policy rate raised 25bp to 1.00% — its highest since 1995 — on a 7–1 vote, with Toichiro Asada dissenting for a hold.
  • The meeting ran without Governor Ueda, who was hospitalized; deputies chaired and fronted the press conference, signaling no rush on the next move.
  • The yen barely firmed (near 160) because the hike was ~90% priced — but the Nikkei breached 70,000 for the first time.

The ECB — its first hike since 2023, live today (June 17)

  • Deposit rate to 2.25%, reversing eight straight cuts, as May eurozone inflation hit 3.2% on war-driven energy costs.
Why it matters: the common shock — the Iran war's oil spike — is now reversing as Brent falls below $80. The puzzle for all three banks is whether to keep fighting an inflation pulse that the de-escalation may itself unwind. Watch the BOJ for carry-trade ripples, and whether Warsh's lighter-guidance Fed actually tilts toward a hike or quietly waits for oil to do the work.

🛢️ The Iran deal at signing eve

where it standsTwo days from the scheduled June 19 signing in Switzerland, the choreography is holding where it can be measured: the US naval blockade is lifted and the first oil tankers have moved back through the Strait of Hormuz. The memorandum — brokered by Pakistan's PM Shehbaz Sharif — opens a 60-day window to negotiate the nuclear file and sanctions relief.

What is confirmed vs. what is deferred

  • Confirmed in practice: Hormuz reopening, the blockade lifted, oil sanctions waived, a signing date and delegation (VP Vance, Witkoff, Kushner reported for the US).
  • Deferred to talks: the actual nuclear file (enrichment, stockpiles), the comprehensive sanctions architecture, and any reconstruction financing — all pushed into the 60-day track.

The one joint that could still snap

The fault line is the Levant. Iran is demanding Israel withdraw from Lebanon; Israel has refused, vowing to hold a roughly 25-mile "security zone" and insisting it is "not a party" to the MOU. Trump publicly told Netanyahu he "has to be more responsible," calling a recent Israeli strike on Beirut "too much." Domestically, the US Senate again rejected an Iran war-powers resolution (47–48) while lawmakers still cannot see the text.

The cleanest live gauge remains oil, not communiqués: Brent below $80 (an early-March low) says the market believes the guns are quiet — but a single incident in Hormuz, or a Friday no-show over Lebanon, could reprice it in minutes. Watch the signing itself, and whether the asset and sanctions mechanics actually move.

🦠 The Ebola Bundibugyo emergency

where it standsThe Central Africa outbreak caused by the Bundibugyo strain of Ebola is still accelerating. As of June 15 the DRC reported 808 confirmed cases and 192 deaths (363 hospitalized in isolation) — up sharply from ~676/136 in mid-May — with Ituri the epicenter (738 cases). Uganda has reported 19 cases and 2 deaths. The WHO declared a PHEIC on May 17.

Why this strain is so hard

  • No approved vaccine or treatment exists for Bundibugyo specifically — the licensed Ebola tools target the Zaire species, leaving responders without their usual frontline defense.
  • The curve is still bending the wrong way: roughly +130 confirmed cases and +56 deaths since mid-May, evidence of active community transmission contact-tracing hasn't contained.
  • Ituri's security and access problems complicate case-finding and safe burials — the classic accelerants of Ebola spread.

The international picture

The G7 in Évian formally called for a coordinated response, and the US has pledged $220M. The metrics to watch: whether Ituri's daily case curve bends, whether the funding actually lands, and — the thing that would change the threat assessment — any first confirmed case outside the DRC/Uganda corridor.
