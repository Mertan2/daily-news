1 · The ledger, not the diplomacy: what "virtually all" actually means

For three weeks this page has argued that the constraint on the Iran campaign was materiel rather than politics. On 4 August that stopped being an inference. Reuters reported that the US Army has expended virtually all of its ATACMS and Precision Strike Missile inventory across five months of Operation Epic Fury. CNN reported the same day that THAAD interceptor stocks are down roughly 80% against pre-war levels and Patriot interceptors roughly half.

The four facts that matter

  1. It is not a theatre problem. A source told Reuters that Central Command has been drawing down stocks pre-positioned elsewhere in the world to reload. The global reserve is shrinking, not being redistributed.
  2. The replacement horizon is years, not months. Even at quadrupled production, meaningful restocking is projected into 2027 or beyond. Nothing announced this week changes 2026.
  3. Rationing is already operational doctrine. Commanders have been choosing which incoming Iranian strikes to defend against. That is not a contingency plan; it is the current posture.
  4. It is one pool, two wars. Patriot is the only system in Ukraine's inventory that can engage ballistic missiles. The Iran campaign and Ukraine's air defence draw on the same interceptor production base.

Point four produced the week's most legible consequence. Overnight into 5 August, Russia fired 24 ballistic missiles, four cruise missiles and 115 drones at Kyiv and its region. Ukraine intercepted zero ballistic missiles. Seventeen people were killed and 44 wounded. Last week Zelensky reported one interception out of 27 and said plainly there were no interceptors left for the Patriot system. This week the number went to zero.

Why this reframes the diplomacy. Every conciliatory move of the past ten days — the cancelled strike package of 1 August, the sixth quiet night, the Omani corridor, the willingness to discuss a reopening without tolls — has been reported as a negotiating posture. The simpler reading is that the negotiating posture is downstream of the ledger. That does not make the diplomacy fake; it makes it necessary, which is a different and more durable thing. It also means the honest leading indicator for whether the war resumes is not rhetoric but production and transfer announcements.

The counter-argument, stated fairly. Trump's Thursday response — "vast quantities of ammunition," record factory construction — is not obviously false. Munitions inventory is compartmented, and "virtually all" of one Army programme is not "out of weapons." Air-delivered standoff munitions, Tomahawk, and Navy stocks are separate lines. What the reporting establishes is narrower and still decisive: the specific classes that do long-range precision strike and ballistic-missile defence are at or near the floor, and those are exactly the two things both wars are consuming.

2 · What a Hormuz "reopening" would actually consist of — and how to check it

The headline this week is that Iran and Oman agreed on geographic coordinates for shipping routes through the Strait, with a joint statement in final drafting and a reported 60-day interim arrangement without tolls that Washington hoped to announce Wednesday. That is a much smaller and much more specific object than "the Strait reopens." It is worth being precise about the parts.

Anatomy of the arrangement

  • Coordinates, not sovereignty. Iran's public position throughout has been that it is administering traffic, not conceding a right of passage it disputes. Agreeing a corridor lets Tehran keep the legal frame ("we are managing shipping") while the practical outcome is transit.
  • No tolls. The toll question was the substantive US red line, because a toll converts a wartime blockade into a permanent rent. Its absence is the real American win, and it is why Washington can sell 60 days.
  • Sixty days is the tell. An interim window short enough that neither side has to describe it as a settlement, and long enough to cover the autumn contracting season.
  • Oman is the counterparty, not the US. Iran continues to deny direct talks with Washington. Every element is routed through Muscat, with Qatar mediating separately and Pakistan engaged alongside.

Three things that could break it inside a fortnight

  1. Tehran's domestic ratification problem. Hardline parliamentarians are compiling impeachment documentation against Foreign Minister Araghchi and have signalled the same may be attempted against President Pezeshkian over the pro-negotiation line. An arrangement that must be described domestically as a routine technical agreement with a neighbour cannot survive an American victory lap. Watch the exact wording of the joint statement.
  2. Attacks that nobody claims. On 4 August the bulk carrier Minoan Pioneer was struck and abandoned; on 6 August a tanker took two explosions nine nautical miles southeast of Khasab. Neither fits a Tehran that wants the corridor to work. Unattributed maritime attacks during a de-escalation are either a control problem inside the IRGC or a third party with an interest in collapse — both are reasons the arrangement is fragile.
  3. The first pause collapsed on a ground event, not a communiqué. The nine-day pause in late July ended when the IRGC hit US forces in Jordan. Nothing about the current architecture prevents a repeat.

The indicator to actually track

Ignore statements. Track transit counts. The series so far: under 10 vessels per week at the height of the closure against roughly 100 in peacetime; 14 on Wednesday 29 July; and the one hard cumulative number published this week — Iraq exported more than 30 million barrels through the Strait between 4 July and 4 August. That figure is the most useful datum of the week, because it is a volume rather than an intention: partial flow was already restored under the pauses, before any agreement. If the corridor is real, weekly transits should move toward the double digits and keep climbing; if the deal is theatre, the Iraq export run-rate stalls.

Secondary confirmation: Brent. It fell a third consecutive session to the $78–79 range on Wednesday, from above $100 in mid-July and $85.48 on 27 July. The market has already priced a substantial reopening. That cuts both ways — the upside from an announcement is small, and the downside from a collapse is large.

3 · The AI capex rule met a rocket company, and held

Two weeks ago this page described a market that had stopped punishing AI capex indiscriminately and started asking one question of each name: does revenue proof travel with the spending? Microsoft and Amazon spent enormously, showed accelerating cloud revenue and were rewarded; Meta spent enormously without new revenue proof and was punished. On 5 August the rule was applied to the hardest possible test case.

The interesting partRevenue doubled and the stock fell 14%

This is the cleanest illustration yet of what the market is actually pricing. SpaceX did not miss. Revenue nearly doubled and losses narrowed — on almost any historic standard a spectacular debut quarter. What broke it was management flagging that the spending cycle behind the ambition is far from over, in a quarter where capex had already gone up sixfold to $18.4bn. The market is no longer rewarding growth financed by capex acceleration unless it can see the terminal shape of the spending. "Revenue doubled" is not an answer to "when does this stop?"

The honest caveat: the same week roughly $101bn of previously locked shares became free to trade. Some meaningful share of that 14% is float mechanics, not a verdict. Anyone reading the print as a pure referendum on AI capex is over-reading it.

The unpriced leg, againFinancing, not capex

This page flagged two weeks ago that the market had learned to price capex but not the financing behind it. Nothing this week improved that. In the background: Nvidia reportedly weighing a $250bn financing guarantee tied to OpenAI's planned Ohio data centre, alongside its investment in Safe Superintelligence; and an Anthropic–AMD capacity agreement for up to 2 GW of MI450/Helios-generation compute with up to $5bn in AMD equity attached. These are vendor-adjacent structures in which the supplier underwrites the customer's ability to buy. They do not appear in a capex line and the current market rule does not test for them. It punished SpaceX for spending it disclosed. It has no instrument yet for spending that is guaranteed rather than booked.

Watch next

  • Nvidia's print — the only name that can falsify the rule, because it is the beneficiary of everyone else's capex rather than a payer.
  • Whether SpaceX recovers as the lockup float is absorbed. If it does, the 14% was mechanics; if it doesn't, the rule bit a doubling-revenue company, and that is a genuine regime marker.
  • Any disclosure regime for vendor financing guarantees. That is the gap.

4 · Türkiye: the softest inflation print since March, and three record lows in the same week

Monday's TÜİK release was, on its face, the best Turkish macro news in months. Annual CPI came in at 31.75%, under the 31.8% consensus and the lowest since March. Monthly was 1.78% — a three-month high, but still below the 1.83% forecast. Then, by Thursday, the lira had set all-time lows against the dollar (47.59), the euro (55.13) and sterling (64.26) in the same session.

Read the composition, not the headline

The disinflation is concentrated in administered and one-off-sensitive categories — utilities down nearly five points, tobacco down nearly five — while the category households buy weekly is going the other way. Food at 37.53% and rising is roughly six points above headline. A print that improves because utility tariff base effects rolled off, while food accelerates, is a statistically real disinflation that will not be experienced as one. That gap is also why the political economy of the 10 September rate decision is harder than the headline number suggests.

The mechanism behind the record lows

The policy rate is frozen at 37% with no scheduled meeting until 10 September — a six-week window in which the central bank cannot act on rates and can only intervene in the currency, which reporting says it did around 47.55. Meanwhile the global dollar index firmed. A frozen nominal rate plus decelerating headline inflation is, mechanically, a rising real rate, which normally supports a currency. It didn't. Three readings, in rough order of how much of the move each explains:

  1. The external account. Last week's data: a $10.4bn monthly trade deficit, up 26.3%, twelve-month deficit at $95.8bn, and Q2 tourism revenue down 2.6% to $15.865bn against a $68bn annual target. The war took a bite out of the one reliably dollar-generating sector.
  2. The calendar. Markets pricing September as the first opportunity to cut — i.e. the first opportunity for carry to deteriorate.
  3. Energy. Brent near $78–79 helps the import bill, but the Iraq pipeline agreement was signed for one year at 750,000 bpd, not five. A hedge, not a resolution.

Signal vs. noise this week

  • Signal: food at 37.53% and accelerating; three simultaneous currency records; gram gold above ₺6,500 at a seven-week high — the domestic hedging instrument moving is a household-behaviour datapoint, not just a commodity print.
  • Noise: BIST 100 closing +0.11% at 13,703. An index level in a depreciating currency is not information about the real economy.
  • Genuinely good: Türk Telekom Q2 net ₺6.0bn against ₺4.9bn expected; Koç Holding ₺19.7bn against ₺13.5bn. Large exporters and FX-earners are beating comfortably. The divergence between corporate earnings and household inflation is the structural story of this cycle.
  • The dated event: 10 September. Nothing else on the Turkish calendar competes with it.

5 · The framework law: what is actually being offered

Tuesday's submission of the Milli Dayanışma ve Toplumsal Bütünleşmenin Güçlendirilmesine Dair Kanun Teklifi to the TBMM presidency with roughly 360 signatures is the first time the "Terror-Free Türkiye" process has produced a legal text rather than a communiqué. The signature list — AK Parti, MHP, DEM Parti, CHP and HÜDA PAR — is broader than any comparable initiative in two decades.

The core mechanism, in plain terms

The twelve articles do not offer amnesty. They offer suspension of prosecution on a sliding scale tied to sentence exposure: five years' suspension for offences carrying under fifteen years, ten years for offences carrying more. That is a structurally different instrument from a pardon — it is revocable, it is time-limited, and it keeps the state's legal claim alive as leverage across an entire decade. Alongside it: verification of actual dissolution and disarmament with all materiel recorded, an MGK decision on the PKK's dissolution to be announced, and a standing parliamentary monitoring commission.

Three specific things to watch, in order

  1. Does the MGK decision actually issue? It is referenced in the text as a step to be announced. Until it does, the disarmament leg is asserted rather than certified.
  2. Where is Yeni Parti? Özgür Özel and 91 deputies left the CHP on 24 July to found it. Last week's published commission seat allocation did not list the party at all, and this week's signature list is reported through the five founding-bloc parties. A process this broad that routes around a 91-deputy bloc carries a legitimacy problem in it — and the discrepancy between the reported post-split parliament and the official seat table is still unresolved.
  3. The pro-government press split. Hürriyet, Sabah and Türkiye ran Thursday's front pages as "historic"; Yeni Şafak gave it markedly less prominence. In this system that divergence typically precedes substantive objections becoming sayable.
