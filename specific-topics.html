1 · The Supreme Court term that redrew presidential power — in both directions

What happened

In the space of two sitting days (June 29–30) the Court handed down a cluster of rulings that, taken together, are the most consequential redefinition of executive authority in a generation — and they did not all point the same way.

Why it matters

The headline is not "the Court sided with Trump" or "against" him — it's that a 6–3 conservative majority drew a deliberate line. It maximized the president's control over the executive bureaucracy (removing a 90-year-old limit that let agencies act independently) while refusing to let that logic swallow either the Federal Reserve or the plain text of the 14th Amendment. Chief Justice Roberts wrote both the Humphrey's reversal and the birthright majority — a signal that this Court is building doctrine, not just picking winners.

The threads to watch

  • Does the Fed's reprieve hold? The 5–4 Cook carve-out is narrow and rests on litigation still moving through lower courts. If it frays, central-bank independence — the single thing markets care about most here — is back in play.
  • How aggressively is the removal power used? At-will control over the FTC, NLRB and EEOC could reshape antitrust, labor and workplace enforcement fast, without new legislation.
  • Midterm money. Loosening coordinated-spending caps changes how much parties can pour directly into 2026 races — an under-covered ruling with large downstream effects.

So what: the presidency got structurally stronger over the bureaucracy, but the Court kept two firewalls — the Fed and birthright citizenship — standing. Watch whether those firewalls survive the follow-on cases.

The number that frames everything: ~$650 billion

That's the aggregate 2026 AI capital spending now projected across the hyperscalers — a figure large enough that a single scare (last week's report that OpenAI might delay its IPO to 2027) briefly knocked ~12% off SoftBank and rattled chip names across Asia. Yet by June 30 that fear had fully reversed: Wall Street closed its best quarter since 2020 (S&P +14%, Nasdaq +20% for Q2), Tokyo climbed back above 70,500, and Korea's June semiconductor exports nearly tripled to a record $44.82B. The trade is intact — but it is now priced for near-perfect execution.

Two systems, two supply chains

The week made the decoupling concrete on both ends:

Despite a February US license to ship H200s, Nvidia has booked zero China revenue as Beijing steers buyers to Huawei, Alibaba, Biren and Moore Threads.

The open question

Everyone is spending as if inference demand will scale to meet the buildout. The bear case isn't that AI is fake — it's timing: does $650B/year of capex earn its return on the schedule that today's valuations assume? The tells to watch are HBM memory pricing (a real-time demand gauge), whether frontier-model gains keep justifying the compute, and whether China's domestic stack matures fast enough to make the export controls moot.

So what: the AI rally survived its first genuine wobble and set record quarterly highs — but it's now a two-track world (a US/allied stack and a walled Chinese one), and the whole edifice rests on capex paying off faster than skeptics think.

3 · The Gulf ceasefire's strange new architecture

A war paused without a table

The most striking feature of this week's US–Iran de-escalation is how it's being run. After the sharpest exchange of the cycle — US strikes on Iranian missile/radar sites, then Iranian fire on US positions in Kuwait and Bahrain — the two sides did not sit down. Instead, US envoys Steve Witkoff and Jared Kushner met Qatar's prime minister in Doha, and Qatar and Pakistan now shuttle between separate US and Iranian technical delegations, implementing a 14-point June memorandum. Oman, meanwhile, tabled a proposal on the future of the Strait of Hormuz.

Why indirect talks, and why they can still work

Neither government can be seen negotiating directly with the other, so a lattice of trusted intermediaries — Qatar, Oman, Pakistan — carries the message traffic and, crucially, lets each side deny it's "talking." The upside: it lowers the political cost of concessions. The downside: it's slow, brittle, and easy to collapse if any mediator's channel breaks.

The three knots

  • UraniumIran's enriched-uranium stockpile is the hardest unresolved issue — the piece most likely to sink a durable deal.
  • Frozen fundsTehran is sending a delegation specifically to pursue unfreezing Iranian assets, its top near-term ask.
  • Lebanon linkageA parallel US-brokered framework would pull the IDF out of southern Lebanon with the Lebanese army overseeing Hezbollah's disarmament — a war that has already killed 4,000+ and displaced over a fifth of Lebanon's population. The two tracks are entangled.

The market's verdict so far is calm: Brent has held steady near $73.50, signaling traders read the mediation as more likely to hold than break. That's the number to watch — a sudden spike would mean the relay has snapped.

So what: the ceasefire is real but load-bearing on intermediaries, not on trust. Its survival probably shows up first in the oil price and in whether Doha yields anything concrete on Hormuz and frozen funds.
