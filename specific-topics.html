1 · The AI-chip whipsaw — a crash, a rebound, and a silicon scramble

Two days that flipped the trade twice

Last week the AI-chip trade got its first real scare and its first real relief in the span of 48 hours. On Thursday, a report that OpenAI had engineered a way to cut AI inference compute by ~50% — reportedly shrinking the GPUs needed to serve ChatGPT from tens of thousands to hundreds — read as a demand warning and gutted Asia's chip-heavy indices. On Friday, the same names roared back on a different report: that Anthropic is in talks with Samsung for a custom AI chip. Same stocks, opposite direction, one day apart.

Why the moves were so violent

Korea's market is unusually exposed to the AI-hardware bet: Samsung and SK Hynix dominate the index to the point that Goldman Sachs estimates each one-percentage-point shift in their combined weight can trigger ~$2 billion of foreign flow. So a story that questions unit demand for memory and GPUs doesn't just move two stocks — it moves the whole tape, in both directions. The buy-side rebound was strong enough to trip a "sidecar" trading halt in Seoul.

The real story: everyone wants their own chip

Underneath the index noise is a structural shift — the biggest AI labs are trying to escape dependence on merchant GPUs by designing custom silicon:

  • AnthropicIn talks with Samsung (2nm process, advanced packaging) for its first custom chip; Samsung was a strategic investor in Anthropic's ~$65B raise, and Anthropic hired ex-OpenAI silicon engineer Clive Chan. (TechCrunch, The Information)
  • OpenAIIts Broadcom-built inference ASIC "Jalapeño" — a reticle-sized chip built in ~9 months — just hit an $18B financing snag: Broadcom will fund only the first 1.3GW phase if Microsoft commits to buying ~40% of output, and Microsoft hasn't signed amid data-center design disputes. Broadcom fell ~4% (−26% from its high). (The Information, Tom's Hardware)
  • The stackOpenAI is separately committed to ~10GW of Nvidia systems, ~6GW from AMD, and ~$300B with Oracle — a targeted ~$600B of capacity that the efficiency report implicitly questions.

The counter-argument: Jevons, not doom

Efficiency has historically increased total consumption of a resource — the Jevons paradox. Cheaper inference could unlock far more AI usage (agents, always-on assistants, real-time video), soaking up freed capacity and then some. A company that truly expected to need fewer chips would not be simultaneously locking in gigawatts of compute and designing its own ASIC. The "~50%" figure is also a reported claim, not a published benchmark — an 8% index crash on one unverified number says more about how crowded the trade was than about proven demand destruction.

Update — Monday, July 6: the rebound held and broadened. The KOSPI added another 2.71% and the Nikkei +0.73%, with strategists saying the move now looks like a genuine recovery rather than a technical bounce — new "floors" are cited near 8,000 (KOSPI) and 70,000 (Nikkei), and buyers are front-running SpaceX's July 7 Nasdaq-100 inclusion. Wall Street reopened firmer (Dow +1.14% to 52,900). Two sessions of gains don't retire the bear case, but they blunt the "unverified ~50% number = demand cliff" read.

So what: The index whipsaw is theatre; the custom-silicon scramble is the plot. Every major lab is trying to own its own chip pipeline — which is bullish for foundries like Samsung and bearish for the assumption that Nvidia captures all the value. Watch HBM pricing and whether Microsoft signs the Jalapeño offtake; those tell you more than any single day's KOSPI print.

2 · NATO in Ankara — why the venue matters as much as the agenda

The set-piece

Tomorrow the alliance's 32 leaders — including Trump — convene at Erdoğan's Presidential Complex for the 36th NATO summit (July 7–8), only the second Türkiye has ever hosted (after Istanbul 2004). On paper the agenda is defense economics: reaffirming an "ironclad" Article 5, finalizing the Hague-set 5%-of-GDP target (3.5% core defense + 1.5% related, by 2035), ramping defense-industrial production, and pledging roughly €70bn to Ukraine for 2026.

Why the location is the story

Hosting a summit is a legitimacy engine, and Erdoğan is using it at a pointed moment. Ankara has been locked down with ~70,000 personnel, air defenses on alert, and a gathering ban running June 28–July 10. A pre-summit security sweep detained 209 people — including alleged ISIS and DHKP-C members, but also lawyers, an academic and a journalist — which HRW called "a crackdown ahead of the NATO summit." The optics of a warm Trump welcome, analysts note (CFR, Washington Post), lend cover just as the domestic CHP leadership crisis heads to a rival July 12 congress and Istanbul's mayor sits in jail.

The bilateral to watch: F-35s

The most consequential meeting may be the Trump–Erdoğan bilateral on Tuesday. Trump has dangled movement on jet engines and Türkiye's return to the F-35 program — from which Ankara was expelled after buying Russia's S-400 air-defense system. The obstacles are real: the S-400s are still in the country, and bipartisan Capitol Hill opposition persists. Erdoğan also meets Zelensky and Syria's al-Sharaa on Wednesday, positioning Türkiye as a hinge between Washington, Kyiv and post-war Damascus.

So what: The communiqué will be about money and Ukraine, but the summit's deeper function is leverage. Türkiye converts NATO's need for a pivotal member into cover at home and a shot at the F-35 abroad. Watch whether the F-35 carrot becomes anything concrete, or stays a carrot — and how much the detention wave shadows the leaders' photos.

3 · Iran buries Khamenei — the succession no one rehearsed

Where the June war landed

The starkest legacy of June's US–Iran war is not a document but an absence. Iran is holding days-long state funeral rites (roughly July 4–9) for Supreme Leader Ayatollah Ali Khamenei, killed by a US–Israeli strike on the war's first day. Tens of thousands packed Tehran on July 5, and his body is slated to travel within Iran and to Iraq. Khamenei led the country for more than three decades; a succession forced by war, rather than managed in advance, is genuinely uncharted territory.

The immediate diplomatic effect

  • PauseThe US agreed to halt denuclearization talks about a week during mourning — a deliberate freeze, not a breakdown. The unresolved knots (uranium stockpiles, frozen funds, the Lebanon linkage) simply wait. The ceasefire rests on a June 17 MOU that reopened the Strait of Hormuz for at least 60 days.
  • The unseen heirThe presumed successor, Khamenei's son Mojtaba Khamenei, had not appeared or spoken publicly as of July 5 — despite posters across Tehran and the funeral's projection of strength. A silent heir at his father's funeral is its own signal about how settled (or not) the transition really is.
  • OilThe war premium has fully drained — markets are pricing exhaustion, not escalation.

The question that decides the region

The Assembly of Experts must now name a successor while the IRGC, rival clerical factions and a bruised security establishment all have stakes in the outcome. The ceasefire that ended the fighting was built around Khamenei's Iran; whether it survives contact with a new leadership — and whether that leadership even wants the nuclear talks to resume — is the single most important variable in the Middle East right now. Every stalled negotiation and every barrel of oil hangs on it.

So what: The war's ceasefire question is settled for the moment. The harder one — what kind of Iran emerges from this funeral, and who actually holds power when the mourning ends — is just beginning. Watch for the Assembly of Experts' timeline and the first public appearance of whoever inherits the office.
