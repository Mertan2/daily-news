1 · The cooling labor market meets a Fed that won't blink

What the data are saying

The US labor market has spent six straight months downshifting, and this week put an exclamation point on it. On July 1, ADP reported just 98,000 private jobs added in June — below the ~110K consensus and down from May's 122K — with nearly all the gains (48K) in education and health and leisure/hospitality adding a token 2,000. The government's more-watched payrolls report was pulled forward to Thursday, July 2 at 8:30am ET (ahead of the July 4 holiday), with forecasters expecting a soft ~100–115K and unemployment holding at 4.3%.

Why this Fed is different

In a normal cycle, a string of sub-150K payroll months would have markets pricing rate cuts. This time they're doing the opposite. Since Kevin Warsh took over as Fed chair (confirmed 54–45 in May), the conversation has flipped from "when do cuts start" to "could the next move be a hike." The reason is on the other side of the mandate: the Fed has lifted its 2026 core-inflation projection to ~3.3% and doesn't see 2% before 2028. That leaves a genuinely awkward setup — a labor market soft enough to worry about, and inflation hot enough that easing looks reckless.

The threads to watch

  • Does "cooling" tip into "contracting"? The tell isn't the headline number — it's revisions, the breadth of hiring beyond health care, and whether the 4.3% jobless rate starts to climb.
  • The September question. A genuinely weak print forces Warsh to choose between his inflation framing and a stalling jobs market. Fed-funds futures will move hard on Thursday's number either way.
  • The dollar feedback loop. A hawkish Fed is a big reason the yen is at a 40-year low and the lira at record lows — so the payrolls print is also, indirectly, an intervention trigger in Tokyo.

So what: for the first time in years, a weak US jobs report may not be a green light for easier money. The Fed has boxed itself in — sticky inflation on one side, a fraying labor market on the other — and markets can no longer assume it rides to the rescue.

2 · The trade order Trump built — and just declined to renew

The deadline that passed

July 1 was the date to renew the USMCA, the North American trade pact that replaced NAFTA — a deal President Trump negotiated, signed, and campaigned on in 2018 as "the best trade deal ever made." His administration let the renewal deadline lapse without renewing, instead opening a formal review with Canada and Mexico. Officials cited the persistent US trade deficit and objected to tariff exemptions that let compliant goods cross duty-free.

What "no renewal" actually means

This is not an overnight collapse. Under the treaty's own terms, failing to jointly reaffirm the pact triggers a years-long review cycle: USMCA stays in force, but a clock starts, and if the three governments can't agree, it would sunset in roughly 10 years. The practical effect is uncertainty — the single thing cross-border supply chains hate most. Automakers, agriculture, and energy trade all run on the assumption of stable, tariff-free North American flows.

Why it fits a larger pattern

  • Leverage over lock-inThe move mirrors the administration's broader instinct — keep counterparties in a state of perpetual renegotiation rather than settling terms. It maximizes leverage but prices in permanent uncertainty.
  • Contrast with the EUNote the split-screen: the same week, the EU deepened its rules-based bloc (Ireland's Council presidency, binding MiCA crypto rules, a common parcel duty) and moved to ratify EU–Mercosur. Two very different theories of how trade should be governed.
  • Currency knock-onThe Canadian dollar and Mexican peso are the first place to watch for market stress; a tariff escalation would feed straight into US import prices — awkward for a Fed already fighting ~3.3% core inflation.

So what: the biggest trade story of the week is a non-event — a deadline that quietly passed. But letting USMCA drift into open-ended review injects durable uncertainty into a $1.5-trillion-plus trade relationship, and it does so just as inflation leaves little room to absorb a tariff shock.

3 · The Gulf ceasefire's machinery — and its first "progress"

A war paused without a table

The most distinctive feature of the US–Iran de-escalation remains how it is run. After the cycle's sharpest exchange — US strikes on Iranian sites, then Iranian fire on US positions in Kuwait and Bahrain — the two governments still do not negotiate directly. Instead, Qatar and Pakistan shuttle between separate US and Iranian technical delegations in Doha, implementing a 14-point June memorandum, while Oman carries a proposal on the future of the Strait of Hormuz.

This week's development

On July 1 the mediators said the process produced "positive progress" and that working groups had been formed — the first concrete forward step since the exchange. US envoys Witkoff and Kushner had met Qatar's PM, and VP JD Vance called the meetings "very good" while adding the pointed caveat that Washington will judge Tehran "by its actions, not its words." Crucially, there was still no high-level, face-to-face meeting — the architecture of deniability held.

The three knots

  • UraniumIran's enriched-uranium stockpile is the hardest unresolved issue and the piece most likely to sink a durable deal.
  • Frozen fundsTehran's top near-term ask is the unfreezing of Iranian assets — a concrete, checkable deliverable that could show whether "progress" is real.
  • Lebanon linkageA parallel framework would pull the IDF from southern Lebanon with the Lebanese army overseeing Hezbollah's disarmament; Tehran treats the two tracks as entangled.

The market's read stayed calm: Brent eased to ~$73 and WTI to ~$68 as the war premium drained out — traders betting the mediation holds. That oil price is the single cleanest real-time verdict on the ceasefire; a sudden spike would mean the relay has snapped.

So what: the ceasefire took its first genuine step forward this week, but it is still load-bearing on intermediaries rather than trust. Watch two numbers — whether frozen funds actually move, and where oil sits — for the earliest sign of whether Doha's "progress" is substance or theater.
