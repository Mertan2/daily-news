The thread in one line: the "Islamabad memorandum" that paused the June US–Iran war was never a peace treaty — it was a 60-day ceasefire built on a single, fragile bargain about who controls the Strait of Hormuz. Over the weekend that bargain failed: the US struck Iran, Iran struck back at US bases in the Gulf, and a deal that looked done a week ago is now a live war again.

What actually happened this weekend

The sequence, as reported across NPR, CNN, CBS, PBS, NBC and Al Jazeera (still developing — treat exact details as provisional):

  • Sat Jun 27US strikes inside Iran. American aircraft hit multiple targets at Trump's direction, citing "continued Iranian aggression against commercial shipping."
  • Sun Jun 28Iran retaliates against US bases. The Revolutionary Guard fired ballistic missiles and drones at the Ali Al Salem airbase in Kuwait and the US Fifth Fleet at Port Salman in Bahrain — the first direct strikes on US facilities this cycle.
  • Sun–MonDiplomacy on the brink. Tehran threatened a "complete halt" to talks; a US official insisted negotiations "will resume"; Trump warned Iran "will no longer exist" if attacks continue.

The key point: this is no longer "will the truce hold?" It is "can a ceasefire be rebuilt after both sides have traded blows on each other's forces?" — a much harder question.

Why a signed deal fell apart so fast

The June accord ended open conflict but deferred every hard question to a 60-day "roadmap" (full-deal target ~Aug 22). Three unresolved seams did the damage:

  • Inspections. The US said Iran agreed to readmit IAEA inspectors "soon"; Iran said only after a final deal. IAEA chief Grossi called the timing "not essential" — language that let each side claim victory and settle nothing.
  • Sequencing & money. Disputes over whether sanctions relief and ~$12–24bn in frozen assets come up front or "pay-for-performance" left the bargain perpetually contestable.
  • Hormuz behavior. The truce hinged on free commercial passage. Iranian drone harassment of shipping is exactly the trigger that the US said it struck over — the seam that finally tore (see below).

A ceasefire with no agreed verification, no agreed sequencing and a contested chokepoint is a ceasefire that runs on goodwill. Goodwill ran out.

The Hormuz lever: why one strait keeps detonating the whole thing

The Strait of Hormuz is the 21-mile-wide neck between Iran and Oman through which roughly a fifth of the world's seaborne oil — and much of its LNG — passes. That geography is the entire game:

  • For Iran it is the one form of leverage that reaches every capital at once: even the threat of closure moves global energy prices and insurance rates.
  • For the US and Gulf states keeping it open is non-negotiable — which is why drone attacks on tankers draw a military response rather than a diplomatic one.
  • The paradox: Iran gains most by threatening Hormuz but loses most by actually closing it (it exports its own crude through the same water, largely to China). So the strait lives in a permanent gray zone of harassment-but-not-closure — and every incident in that gray zone can be read as a violation.

That is why a deal "about" nuclear enrichment keeps living or dying on tanker incidents hundreds of miles away.
