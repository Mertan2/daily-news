🛢️ The Hormuz shock — how a strait moved US inflation

The single most consequential chain of the week runs from a waterway to your gas pump to the Fed's June meeting. When the April Iran ceasefire collapsed and the Strait of Hormuz stayed largely closed to shipping — roughly a fifth of the world's seaborne oil normally passes through it — the supply premium showed up almost immediately in prices.

Why it matters

  • mechanismEnergy is a near-universal input. A jump in crude flows through to gasoline, airfares, freight and, with a lag, almost every physical good — which is why a single geopolitical chokepoint can lift a whole inflation index. The BLS said energy alone drove 60%+ of May's increase.
  • the splitHeadline 4.2% vs. core 2.9%. Core (ex-food/energy) rose just 0.2% on the month. That gap is the whole debate: an energy shock is, in theory, a one-off level shift the Fed can "look through" — unless it bleeds into wages and expectations and becomes self-sustaining.
  • the trapStagflation risk. An oil shock both raises prices (argues for hikes) and dents growth (argues for cuts). With the labor market still firm, markets have started pricing the tail risk that the Fed's next move is a hike — even as it is expected to hold at the June 16–17 meeting while it waits to see if the spike sticks.

The tell to watch

Brent is the cleanest real-time gauge. A reopening of Hormuz would unwind much of the inflation and market damage within weeks; continued closure entrenches it — the EIA's closure scenario points to a ~$105 June–July average. Trump's "total victory in two weeks, oil prices come tumbling down" is, in effect, a bet on the former.

Plain-English glossary — CPI: the consumer price index, the headline measure of how fast prices rise. Core CPI: the same, minus volatile food and energy, used to read the underlying trend. Stagflation: high inflation plus stagnant growth — the worst case for a central bank, because the two problems call for opposite medicine.

🤖 The AI IPO wave — Anthropic files, Nvidia sells "Vera"

Even amid a macro shock, the AI complex remains the market's swing factor — and this month it took a defining step toward the public markets.

  • filingAnthropic filed confidentially for an IPO (June 1) at a reported ~$965B valuation, with its revenue run-rate jumping from roughly $10B to ~$47B in about a year — one of the steepest revenue ramps the software industry has seen. A confidential filing lets a company start the SEC review privately before committing to a public timetable.
  • siliconNvidia named Anthropic, OpenAI and SpaceX as first users of its new "Vera" CPU (full production in Q3) — a push to pair its GPUs with its own general-purpose processors and capture more of the data-center stack.
  • counter-currentAt the same time, the big AI buyers are exploring custom in-house chips to control compute cost and supply — a long-term challenge to Nvidia's share even as overall demand grows. The early-June semiconductor rout (Nasdaq's worst day since April 2025) was the market chewing on exactly this tension.

Why it matters

An Anthropic listing at this scale would be a landmark test of how public investors value frontier-AI economics — enormous revenue growth against enormous compute spend. It also concentrates index risk: a handful of AI names now drive a large share of US market moves, which is why an AI wobble and a Middle East oil shock can hit the tape on the same day.

Run-rate: annualizing recent revenue (e.g. last month × 12) to express current scale — useful for fast-growing firms, but it flatters a company still accelerating. Confidential filing: an SEC process (allowed for many issuers) to begin review privately and only go public with the prospectus closer to the offering.

🦠 Bundibugyo-Ebola — why this outbreak is hard to stop

The DRC–Uganda outbreak keeps climbing, and the reason it is unusually stubborn is specific: the pathogen is Bundibugyo virus, one of the rarer Ebola species — and the licensed Ebola vaccine (Ervebo) targets the Zaire species, so its cross-protection here is judged insufficient.

  • no matched vaccineThere is no approved Bundibugyo-specific vaccine or therapeutic. CEPI has put ~$62M behind three candidates (Moderna mRNA, Oxford ChAdOx1, IAVI), but trials are months away — so the front line is still classic outbreak control: case isolation, contact tracing and safe burials.
  • geographyIturi province is the epicenter (563 of the DRC's confirmed cases across 17 health zones), with spread into North and South Kivu and cross-border transmission into Uganda among contacts and health workers — a dense, mobile, conflict-affected region that makes tracing hard.
  • responseWHO declared a PHEIC on May 16–17; on June 5 Africa CDC and WHO launched a joint continental plan seeking US$518M. The metric that matters most is contact-tracing completeness — it needs to climb well above 90% to bend the curve.

PHEIC (Public Health Emergency of International Concern): the WHO's highest alarm level, which unlocks coordinated international response. Cross-protection: when a vaccine made for one strain partly works against a related one — here judged too weak to rely on.

🇨🇳 The Xi–Kim summit — read the silence

Xi Jinping's June 8–9 state visit to Pyongyang — his first trip abroad in 2026 — produced warm language about "powerful momentum" and a friendship that "will not change." But the most telling detail was an omission: the official Xinhua readout made no mention of denuclearization.

  • the signalFor years, China's stated line included Korean-peninsula denuclearization. Dropping it from a flagship readout — days after North Korea unveiled a third uranium-enrichment plant — reads as Beijing implicitly accepting the North as a nuclear state, and prioritizing the relationship over the old non-proliferation framing.
  • the strategyAnalysts frame the visit as Xi keeping Pyongyang anchored to China rather than letting it drift fully into Russia's orbit (the two leaders met May 19–20). It's a three-body problem — Beijing, Moscow and Pyongyang — in which China wants to remain the indispensable partner.
  • the leverage playIn parallel, Xi is pressing Trump to curb US arms sales to Taiwan, and Beijing has stayed conspicuously quiet on Taiwan — restraint that analysts read as avoiding anything that would galvanize US support. The Korea and Taiwan files are being played as one board.

Watch the US and South Korean responses, any North Korean weapons tests, and whether the no-denuclearization framing hardens into open policy. If it does, it marks a real shift in how the region's nuclear question is even discussed.
