The thread in one line: for 91 years, a president could not fire the people who run agencies like the FTC, FCC and NLRB just for disagreeing with them — that was the whole point of calling them "independent." On June 29 the Supreme Court ended that, 6–3. The president can now remove most of them at will. The one place the Court drew a line was the Federal Reserve.

What the Court actually did

In a 6–3 decision, the justices overturned Humphrey's Executor v. United States (1935), the precedent that let Congress create agencies whose leaders could only be fired "for cause" — inefficiency, neglect or malfeasance — rather than at the president's pleasure. The holding, in plain terms:

  • beforeA president who disliked the direction of, say, the Federal Trade Commission had to wait out the commissioners' fixed terms. Removing one required a real, documentable cause.
  • afterThe president may now remove the heads of most independent agencies at will — the same way a cabinet secretary serves "at the pleasure of the president."
  • whyThe majority's logic is the "unitary executive" theory: Article II vests all executive power in the president, so anyone exercising that power must ultimately answer to — and be removable by — the president.

This lands on the same day the Court is closing its term, with the birthright-citizenship case still outstanding. It is one of the broadest rulings on the shape of the executive branch in living memory.

Why a 1935 case was holding up the modern state

Humphrey's Executor arose when FDR tried to fire an FTC commissioner he disagreed with. The Court said no — and in doing so, it made the modern "independent agency" constitutionally possible. That single idea quietly underwrites a huge slice of how the US is governed:

  • Markets & competition — the FTC and SEC police monopolies, fraud and disclosure, insulated (in theory) from election-cycle pressure.
  • Labor — the NLRB referees union elections and unfair-labor-practice disputes.
  • Communications & airwaves — the FCC sets the rules for broadcast, broadband and spectrum.
  • Consumer finance & more — a long list of multi-member boards built on the assumption that "for-cause" protection would survive.

The case for independence was that some decisions — interest rates, antitrust enforcement, who wins a contested union vote — work better when they're not a direct extension of whoever just won the White House. The case against it, which the majority adopted, is that unelected officials wielding real power with no accountability to the elected president is itself a democratic problem. Tuesday's ruling resolves a tension that's been latent since 1935 — decisively, in favor of presidential control.

The one carve-out: the Federal Reserve survives, 5–4

Here's the twist that keeps this from being a clean sweep. In a separate 5–4 ruling, the Court refused to let the president immediately remove Fed Governor Lisa Cook, holding she can stay in her seat until the underlying litigation plays out in the lower courts.

  • Why the Fed is different. The majority signaled that the central bank — which sets interest rates and is uniquely sensitive to political interference — sits in a special constitutional category, distinct from ordinary regulatory agencies.
  • Why it's narrow. A 5–4 split (versus the 6–3 on the main ruling) means the Fed's protection rests on a thinner margin — and the Court resolved it procedurally (Cook stays "for now") rather than with an airtight doctrinal shield.
  • Why markets cared. A president able to fire Fed governors at will is the nightmare scenario for bond and currency markets, because it threatens the credibility of inflation-fighting. Monday's strong rally — Dow above 52,000 for the first time — partly reflects relief that the Fed line held.

So the picture is: maximal presidential control over most of the administrative state, with the central bank fenced off — for now, by one vote.

Why this reaches past Washington

This sounds like an inside-the-Beltway doctrine fight. It isn't. The agencies in question make decisions that hit prices, paychecks and portfolios:

AgencyWhat it controlsWho feels a change of direction
FTC / SECAntitrust, mergers, market fraudConsumers, investors, anyone in a concentrating industry
NLRBUnion elections, labor disputesWorkers and employers
FCCBroadband, broadcast, spectrumInternet users, media, telecoms
Federal ReserveInterest rates, bank supervisionBorrowers, savers, every market — shielded, 5–4

The deeper shift is about volatility. Independent agencies were designed to change slowly and survive elections. If their leadership can now turn over with each administration, regulatory policy in finance, labor and tech could swing much harder from term to term — raising the political risk premium baked into long-horizon business decisions.
