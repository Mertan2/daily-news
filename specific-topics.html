1 · One interceptor stock, two wars — the arithmetic stops closing

On Saturday, two stories ran on opposite sides of the world and were treated as unrelated. They are the same story.

Story A: Kyiv, before dawn

Russia fired 35 missiles at Ukraine, 27 of them ballistic, plus 185 drones. Nine people died in Kyiv, 27 to 30 were injured, four of them children. Zelensky's explanation of why so little was stopped was unusually flat: only one ballistic missile was intercepted, because there are no interceptors left for the Patriot system.

Story B: the Pentagon, the same week

Gen. Alexus Grynkewich, commander of US European Command, sent written notification to senior Pentagon officials that without another Navy destroyer he will have to choose defence of the American homeland over defence of Israel against Iranian ballistic missiles. Five destroyers deploy from Rota, Spain; a sixth arrives later this year. As of Friday, two were in the Mediterranean and three were alongside in port. Five months of war have raised maintenance demand and consumed interceptor inventory.

Both stories are about the same finite thing: the number of interceptors the United States can produce and the number of platforms it has to fire them from. Ukraine and Israel are drawing on that pool at once, and a third claim — the homeland — has just been asserted as senior to both.

Why this reframes the war coverage

For five months the ceiling on the Iran conflict has been described politically: what Trump wants, what Tehran will accept, what the midterms require. This week produced the first explicit statement from a combatant commander that the ceiling is materiel. That changes what the pauses mean.

  • A pause announced while the other side is still flying drones (Kuwait, Saturday) is not a de-escalation. It may be a resupply interval.
  • Bloomberg's earlier reporting on US munitions-stock constraints bounded the tempo of strikes. The Grynkewich memo bounds the defensive side, which is the harder constraint — you can choose not to strike, but you cannot choose not to be struck.
  • Ukraine's air defence has moved from doctrine-limited to stock-limited. A 1-in-27 ballistic intercept rate is not a tactical result; it is an inventory readout.

What would actually resolve this

  • Production rates, not deployments. Watch PAC-3 MSE and SM-3/SM-6 procurement and any supplemental request. Hull movements are noise; line rates are signal.
  • The sixth Rota destroyer's arrival date. Grynkewich framed the problem as one hull. If that hull arrives early, the memo was leverage. If it does not, the memo was a warning.
  • Whether Israel's Iron Dome transfer question reopens. Netanyahu blocked transferring a system to Ukraine this week. That decision looks different if American interceptors are the binding constraint for both.
  • Hormuz transit counts. Still the honest indicator of whether anything has actually de-escalated — fewer than 10 ships in a week at the worst of it, 14 on one day mid-week, roughly 100 in peacetime.
Care with this one. The Grynkewich memo is reported from officials describing a private written communication, not a published document. The interceptor-shortage inference linking the two theatres is analysis on this page, not a claim made by either government. Neither Washington nor Kyiv has stated a unified stock figure, and none is public.
Sources: Washington Post · Kyiv Independent · NPR · Haaretz · CNN

The tourism number is the one that matters, and not for the obvious reason

A 2.6% revenue decline is small. What it conceals is the shape: visitor numbers fell while revenue was largely protected. That is a market trading volume for yield — charging more to fewer people. Marmaris prices rose 22.7% in sterling terms and it dropped to seventh in destination rankings.

Turkish analysts name three causes: the Gulf crisis, domestic cost inflation, and a lost price advantage. The third is the structural one. Türkiye's tourism model has historically been price-competitive against Greece, Spain and Egypt. Domestic inflation running above 32% while the lira is defended erodes that advantage in exactly the currencies the customers hold.

Tourism is Türkiye's largest source of non-debt hard currency. A season that undershoots is not a sector story — it is a balance-of-payments story arriving in the same quarter as a widening trade deficit and an oil price back at $90.

What went right this week

The Iraq–Turkey Crude Oil Pipeline agreement, signed this weekend, maintains 750,000 bpd for one year while long-term terms are negotiated. With Hormuz functioning intermittently, Ceyhan is one of the few crude corridors out of the region not passing through a contested strait — that is transit revenue and strategic relevance at the same time. The one-year term is itself informative: neither side will price a multi-year deal mid-war.

The EBRD and company-formation numbers are also genuinely good, and worth holding against the gloom. Roughly a third of new firms are in Istanbul, with a significant share foreign-partnered — particularly Syrian and Iranian.

The political clock, running alongside

  • Erdoğan told the AK Party's provincial council on Saturday that the Terror-Free Türkiye process had come this far "without any road accident" and will "reach its destination." Party membership: 11.71 million.
  • The commission seated Friday has 48 members and still does not list Yeni Parti — the party Özgür Özel founded on 24 July with 91 deputies who left the CHP. Three days on, that discrepancy with the post-split parliament remains unexplained.
  • Trade Minister Ömer Bolat is pressing for Customs Union modernisation with the EU, citing $233bn in bilateral trade and naming automotive as the urgent case.

What to watch

The August CPI print; whether Yeni Parti registers a parliamentary group; Q3 tourism bookings, which are set now; and whether Brent stays at $90 through the six-week window in which the central bank has no scheduled tool.

3 · The AI trade learned to price capex. It has not learned to price the financing.

The thing the rule does not touch

While the market was busy grading income statements, the financing structure underneath moved a great deal further:

  • Nvidia is negotiating roughly $250bn in financial guarantees backing OpenAI's lease of a 10-gigawatt campus at Piketon, Ohio — built by SoftBank subsidiary SB Energy, up to 800MW by 2028, total project cost above $500bn.
  • A parallel negotiation would finance up to $350bn of GPU purchases by OpenAI, taking Nvidia's exposure to a single customer's single project toward $600bn.
  • Industry-wide AI infrastructure spend is projected above $700bn in 2026 and $3tn cumulatively through 2028.
A chip vendor guaranteeing its largest customer's ability to buy that vendor's chips is vendor financing. At $600bn it is vendor financing at a scale where the vendor's revenue quality and the customer's solvency stop being independent variables.

This is not a prediction that it fails. Vendor financing works whenever demand compounds faster than the guarantee amortises, and AI demand has done exactly that for three years. The observation is narrower: the discrimination the market applied this week operated on reported revenue, and these structures do not appear in reported revenue. Microsoft's disclosure that two-thirds of its capex is short-lived assets — CPUs and GPUs — is the kind of detail that matters here, because depreciation schedules are where the optimism gets stored.

The other split: open weights and security

  • Jensen Huang's open letter arguing that open-weight models are vital to competition and US technological leadership now has ~50 signatories, including OpenAI, Microsoft, Meta and Google. Amazon and Anthropic did not sign.
  • Nvidia's separate Open Secure AI Alliance — 30-plus companies building open cybersecurity tooling for defenders, formed after an OpenAI agent breach — is missing OpenAI, Google and Anthropic.
  • Chinese open-weight models continue gaining traction with US developers, which is the competitive fact the open-weights letter is really about.

Two coalitions, two different absentee lists, and no company on both sides of the same line. Worth watching as a map of who expects to compete on distribution versus on capability.

What to watch

Nvidia reports 26 August — not this week. Before then: HBM pricing, whether Meta's capex floor moves again, and whether any of the OpenAI financing structures get disclosed in a filing rather than reported by wires.

4 · The Ebola outbreak doubled during the week nobody covered it

This thread has been on the page for three editions with the same complaint: severity far exceeding coverage. This week the numbers moved enough that the complaint needs restating with figures rather than adjectives.

The facts, in the order that matters

  • Reported in Ituri Province, DRC, on 14 May — the country's 17th Ebola outbreak, beginning five months after the previous one ended.
  • Declared a PHEIC on 16 May. That was eleven weeks ago.
  • It is Bundibugyo ebolavirus. Both licensed vaccines — Ervebo, and the Mvabea/Zabdeno regimen — target Zaire. WHO does not consider them effective against this species. Licensed monoclonals were certified against Zaire too.
  • That leaves isolation, contact tracing and supportive care. In practice: nineteenth-century tools against a virus with a 44% case fatality ratio.
  • It is now the fastest-growing Ebola outbreak on record.
  • Uganda: 20 confirmed cases, two deaths, plus one probable case who died.
  • Exported cases: France confirmed one on 24 June in a doctor returning from the DRC; a US citizen was evacuated to Germany in May. The CDC has renewed travel restrictions through mid-August.

What changed this week — the first genuinely hopeful item

  • 12 July: clinical trials began in the DRC for remdesivir and MBP134, a monoclonal cocktail designed for broader ebolavirus coverage.
  • The University of Oxford has been cleared to begin Phase 1 trials of a candidate vaccine.
  • 22 July: the New England Journal of Medicine published a study of cross-reactive Bundibugyo antibody responses in people who received the licensed Zaire-targeting vaccines — the question of whether existing stockpiles offer any partial protection.
A Phase 1 trial beginning eleven weeks into an outbreak that has already killed 1,587 people is not a rescue. It is a bet on the outbreak after this one. The honest framing is that the countermeasure gap for non-Zaire species was known, was not closed, and is now being closed under fire.

Why it belongs on a markets-and-politics page

Two exported cases have already reached high-income countries. Neither seeded onward transmission. The relevant question is not whether Bundibugyo will "reach the West" — it has, twice — but what the response looks like when the tools in the stockpile are the wrong ones. The CDC's rolling travel restrictions are a policy instrument being used because the pharmaceutical instrument does not exist.

Figures move. WHO, CDC, ECDC and national health ministries publish on different cadences and with different confirmed/probable definitions; the numbers above are confirmed cases and reported deaths and will be revised. Case fatality ratios computed mid-outbreak are biased by reporting lag in both directions.
