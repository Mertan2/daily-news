2. Neurological Computers

Paradromics' Connexus implant on Thursday made the BCI field a two-horse race. Single-neuron-resolution recording at >200 bps puts Paradromics on the same field as Neuralink's three-continent trial cohort.

Paradromics completed its first in-human implant of the Connexus BCI on May 14 at the University of Michigan. The device, smaller than a dime, holds 420 micro-needle electrodes and was placed in the temporal lobe of an epilepsy patient already in scheduled neurosurgery — implanted, recorded from, and removed within ~20 minutes. Surgeon: Dr. Oren Sagher. Research lead: Dr. Matthew Willsey. The Connexus records at single-neuron resolution and demonstrated >200 bits/second bandwidth — roughly 20× the bandwidth of Neuralink's initial pick-and-place demo. The temporal-lobe target is critical for auditory processing and memory — long-term implants there are the path to speech-restoration BCIs that Paradromics has FDA clearance to pursue.

The Neuralink picture is closer to scale. April disclosures: 45 patients enrolled across 3 continents; 85% of spinal-cord-injury participants are hitting pick-and-place performance within 150% of non-injured baselines. The next-gen Neuralink surgical robot is now capable of reaching virtually any brain region. Operationally Neuralink remains ahead on patient count; technically Paradromics has just shown the higher-resolution recording stack.

The neuromorphic side: Cambridge's hafnium-oxide neuromorphic chip claim from May 13 (70% AI-energy cut on memory-compute workloads) continues to pick up second-day citations. The argument: separating compute from memory is the fundamental architectural limit on power-per-FLOP for current GPU-based AI; hafnium-oxide RRAM crossbar arrays let memory be compute. If the energy claim holds in independent replications, it materially changes the data-centre buildout math now driving the $725B Big-Tech 2026 capex curve.

3. Quantum Computing

A genuinely dense week: Kyoto's W-state readout unblocks scalable photonic networks, Jülich/NVIDIA crack 50 simulated qubits, ETH stabilises neutral atoms, and Fujitsu opens a joint research cluster with Science Tokyo on May 15.

The stack of this week's breakthroughs: (1) Kyoto University + Hiroshima University on May 13 built a tabletop photonic circuit that performs one-shot identification of 3-photon W states by exploiting cyclic shift symmetry through a photonic quantum-Fourier-transform circuit. The method generalises to any number of photons — unblocking a long-standing bottleneck in quantum-network verification. (2) Jülich Supercomputing Centre + NVIDIA on May 11 announced full simulation of a 50-qubit universal quantum computer — smashing the previous 48-qubit classical-simulation record. (3) ETH Zürich on May 12 demonstrated neutral-atom qubits stable under operation, holding coherence at depths that bring fault-tolerant gates within practical reach. (4) Fujitsu × Institute of Science Tokyo on May 15 launched the "Fujitsu Quantum and HPC Infrastructure Collaborative Research Cluster" — full-stack research from hardware design through control and evaluation, plus a quantum-engineer talent pipeline for Japan.

The standing useful-advantage anchor case remains Q-CTRL × IBM's 3,000× speedup on 120-qubit Fermi-Hubbard materials simulation (with runtime error suppression). Google Willow's "Quantum Echoes" 13,000× over classical sits in Nature. Harvard researchers stated this week the field is "advancing faster than expected." IBM's "Decade of Quantum on the Cloud" anchors the broader operational trajectory.

The pattern: error rates are dropping faster than qubit counts are rising, and the verification stack — long the bottleneck for quantum networks — is now keeping pace. Industry watchers are calling this the year quantum officially entered the "fault-tolerant foundation era," the threshold where adding qubits actually reduces noise rather than amplifies it.

4. New Spreading Virus

The MV Hondius hantavirus cluster is stable at 11 cases / 3 deaths. No new clusters anywhere. The ship docks the Netherlands May 17–18 — the final logistic milestone of the response phase.

WHO DON601 / CDC HAN00528 Level 3 hold today. As of the latest WHO update (May 13–14 window), the multi-country Andes hantavirus cluster traced to the MV Hondius cruise is at 11 cases (8 confirmed + 2 probable + 1 inconclusive) and 3 deaths. Two additional confirmed cases since May 8 — one in France, one in Spain. No community-spread events anywhere. The working hypothesis remains that the index case acquired the infection prior to boarding, likely from a land-based exposure on the Ushuaia leg.

The ship: MV Hondius departed Ushuaia, Argentina, on April 1 and visited Antarctica, South Georgia Island, Tristan da Cunha, Saint Helena and Ascension Island carrying 147 passengers and crew from 23 countries. It arrived Tenerife on May 11; passenger disembarkation and repatriation were completed by ECDC's update of May 13. Docking in the Netherlands May 17–18 closes the ship-side logistics. The Andes virus is the only hantavirus known to spread human-to-human, typically by close, sustained contact — fatality rate approximately 40%, incubation up to 8 weeks.

US response: 15 repatriated passengers are isolated at the National Quarantine Unit in Nebraska; no US-confirmed cases. Five California residents are under monitoring as exposed contacts. CDC: "public health risk in the United States is extremely low." WHO's Maria Van Kerkhove: "This is not the start of a COVID pandemic." Tedros earlier in the week: "not another Covid."
