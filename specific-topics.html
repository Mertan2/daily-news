1 · The AI-capex repricing reaches America

Markets For two weeks the AI-trade selloff was an Asian story with an Asian cause. Last week TSMC posted its best quarter in history and the stock fell anyway; the Nikkei dropped 4%, and Kioxia halved from its June peak, erasing roughly ¥30tn. The bear case had stopped being about DeepSeek's cheaper inference chip and become something harder to argue with: a multiple-compression story, where even record earnings can't lift a stock because the market is repricing expectations, not fundamentals.

This week the same question crossed the Pacific. On July 22, Alphabet and Tesla both beat on revenue and fell after hours — the clearest sign yet that the doubt is now aimed at the American names, not just the memory makers.

The tell: Alphabet's Google Cloud grew 82% to $24.8bn and total revenue beat at $119.8bn — and the stock still dropped, on a raised capex forecast. When more AI spending is punished rather than rewarded, the market is no longer asking whether demand is real; it's asking when the spending pays back.

Why it matters

The AI build-out is real — global venture funding hit a record $510bn in H1 2026, more than 70% of Q2 capital going to AI. The market isn't doubting the demand; it's doubting the return on the capex, and that doubt is contagious across the whole stack: memory (Kioxia, SK Hynix, CXMT's new $9.8bn IPO), foundry (TSMC), and now the hyperscalers writing the cheques. Watch SK Hynix on July 29 and the rest of Big Tech earnings for whether "spend more" keeps being met with a lower share price.

The governance backdrop

Underneath the trade, the rules for AI are splitting into blocs. Shanghai's WAICO — 29 countries, no US or major EU presence — was founded July 16 opposite Brussels' order forcing Google to open Android and share Search data. Two incompatible theories of control now have institutions behind them, and the capex question and the governance question increasingly point at the same companies.

2 · Iran — a war into its fourth week, and the oil premium returns

Security The June ceasefire — conditioned on reopening the Strait of Hormuz — collapsed, and by July 22 CENTCOM reported an eleventh consecutive night of strikes on Iranian military facilities. The war has now taken its first American lives: two service members killed in Jordan around July 17–18, one missing, with roughly 100 personnel suffering mostly minor injuries since early July.

The new variable is the oil premium. Brent surged to about $94–95 — its highest since early June — on the strikes plus Houthi claims of hitting two Saudi tankers in the Red Sea. Hormuz carries roughly a fifth of the world's seaborne oil and remains effectively shut. That premium is what turns a regional war into a global inflation problem.

Where the pressure points are

  • The money: Secretary of War Hegseth put the campaign's cost at ~$38bn and asked Congress for a nearly $88bn supplemental (figures single-sourced at compile time).
  • The politics: Schumer said Democrats will force another war-powers vote "to end this unauthorized war"; a prior version cleared the Senate 50–48 in late June, but Congress hasn't managed to bind the administration.
  • The diplomacy: Pakistan and Qatar floated a 10-day ceasefire; Washington downplayed it, with Rubio insisting any deal guarantee freedom of navigation through Hormuz. Turkey's Erdoğan called the strikes "unlawful" and pitched Ankara as a mediator.

Why it reprices everything else

Every other thread on this page runs through the oil price. Japan's yen weakness and Korea's rate hike are partly Mideast-energy stories; Turkey's central bank explicitly cited external risk; the Fed meets July 28–29 with $95 oil in the frame. As long as Hormuz stays shut with no stated endpoint, this is the single biggest cross-asset variable.

3 · Turkey — an opposition splitting under judicial pressure

Politics The largest domestic story of the Turkey week is a fracture inside the main opposition. On July 21, Özgür Özel used what he called his last CHP group address to announce he and loyalist deputies are leaving to found a new party, its name to be revealed only when the founding petition is filed on July 27. Reports put possible defections at 75–82 deputies — enough to make the new party the main opposition and drop the court-restored CHP to fourth or fifth in parliament.

The trigger: a leadership case with no resolution until September

This is not a normal breakaway; it's an escape from a legal trap. The "mutlak butlan" ruling voided the 2023 congress that elected Özel and reinstated Kılıçdaroğlu's old administration as caretakers. The appeal reached the Court of Cassation this week, but the judicial recess pushes any decision to September — leaving the CHP with a contested "two-headed" leadership for two more months. Rather than risk losing the party outright, Özel is building a new vehicle he controls.

The "who benefits" fight: a Cumhuriyet poll placed the new party first, but CHP figures warn the split serves Erdoğan — spokesperson Müslim Sarı said "we will not allow Atatürk's party to be split." International outlets (FT, Reuters, AP) largely read it as fracturing the opposition in the short term.

The pressure around it

  • The arrest wave: İzmit's mayor detained July 20 among 31; Çankaya's mayor jailed earlier; an Eyüpsultan operation July 22 — now 40+ CHP municipalities since the İmamoğlu detention of March 2025. Opposition calls it a purge; the government calls it anti-corruption.
  • The economy: the TCMB held at 37% on July 23 with no August meeting, so the stance carries to September 10; the lira sits near 47.2/USD and June CPI ran 32.11%.
  • The peace process: the "terror-free Turkey" initiative stalled, with the PKK insisting any legislation secure Öcalan's release and the government non-committal.
Caveats: deputy-defection counts and poll standings are the softest numbers here and are fluid; the new party's name, exact size and launch date won't be confirmed until the July 27 filing.
