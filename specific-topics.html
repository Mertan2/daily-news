🇬🇧 Britain's revolving door: why Starmer fell, and what Burnham inherits

what happenedOn Monday, Keir Starmer resigned as prime minister and Labour leader after a parliamentary-party revolt, becoming caretaker until a successor is chosen. He is set to be Britain's seventh prime minister in ten years — a churn (Cameron → May → Johnson → Truss → Sunak → Starmer → next) with no modern precedent. The proximate trigger was Andy Burnham's June 18 Makerfield by-election win, which put the popular ex-Manchester mayor back in the Commons and made him eligible to lead. Burnham declared within hours; his most plausible rival, Wes Streeting, stood aside and endorsed him.

Why a by-election toppled a sitting PM

  • The challenger had to be in Parliament. Burnham's support among Labour MPs had been building for weeks, but a leadership bid was impossible while he sat in Manchester's town hall. Makerfield removed the one structural barrier — and turned latent discontent into a live alternative overnight.
  • The defence-spending fight had already drawn blood. Starmer's push to hit NATO's higher spending targets — sharpened by Hegseth's "NATO 3.0" pressure — triggered earlier MoD resignations and split the party's left. The Iran-war energy shock and a squeezed cost of living did the rest.
  • Labour did the math. Once a credible, electable rival existed, MPs facing the next general election preferred to change leader now rather than defend a wounded one. Starmer "heard the answer of my parliamentary party… and accepted it with good grace."

What a Burnham government would mean

  • A tilt to the soft left. Burnham has run to Starmer's left on public ownership, workers' rights and regional devolution. Expect a rhetorical reset toward "the red wall" and away from the technocratic centrism that defined Starmerism.
  • A Treasury question mark. Chancellor Rachel Reeves is widely seen as unlikely to survive the transition — so the market's first question is who runs fiscal policy, and whether the spending envelope loosens. Sterling and gilts will trade the answer.
  • A fast, possibly uncontested, handover. Nominations open July 9 and close July 16; if Burnham is the only candidate to clear the threshold, the contest ends there and he could take office around mid-to-late July — with a Manchester mayoral by-election on July 30 to follow.
Why it matters: a G7 economy is changing leaders mid-parliament for the seventh time in a decade, just as Europe debates a "NATO with less America" and the Ankara summit nears (July 7–8). The continuity questions — Ukraine support, the 5%-of-GDP defence path, the fiscal rules — now run through one man's leadership platform. Watch the nomination count (a coronation vs a contest), Reeves's fate, and the first gilt-market reaction to a Burnham economic prospectus.

🛢️ Two versions of one deal: inside the US–Iran "60-day roadmap"

the setupThe first round of Bürgenstock talks ended Monday with a "roadmap" to a final deal within 60 days — a real, dated process where days earlier there was only a postponed ceremony. Yet within hours, Washington and Tehran were describing different agreements. That gap is not noise; it is the deal's central design feature, and its central risk.

Where the two accounts diverge

  • Inspectors. VP Vance said Iran agreed to readmit IAEA inspectors "as early as this week" and to discuss disposing of highly enriched uranium — "a major milestone." Tehran's foreign ministry (Baghaei) said any IAEA engagement stays within existing safeguards, with no new commitments. Both can be technically true at once — which is precisely the point.
  • The money. Speaker Ghalibaf told an Iranian audience the US agreed to release $12bn in two $6bn tranches (the first for food and medicine). A senior US official told American reporters that upfront access is "completely not true" — the structure is "pay-for-performance." Note the drift from earlier $24bn draft-text reports.
  • The shopping list. Trump said released funds would buy US farm goods; Iran's central-bank governor said Iran "has no obligation to buy" anything.

Why "constructive ambiguity" is doing the work

  • Each leader needs a different story. Trump needs "inspections and leverage"; Iran's negotiators need "sanctions relief without nuclear surrender." A roadmap with milestones — rather than a signed, specified treaty — lets both sell a win at home today and fight over the details later.
  • The confidence-builders are concrete; the hard parts are deferred. What actually shipped this round was operational: a Hormuz communication line, an Israel–Lebanon deconfliction cell, and a US move to ease oil sanctions through Aug 21. The genuinely contentious files — enrichment caps, verification, the asset mechanism — were handed to working groups.
  • The spoiler sits outside the room. Trump is simultaneously threatening to "hit Iran very hard again" over Hezbollah, and Israel's Smotrich says troops won't leave south Lebanon "as long as Hezbollah exists." The clause that nearly sank the ceremony — "on all fronts including Lebanon" — is the one Washington can't unilaterally deliver.
Why it matters: ambiguity is what got a deal at all, but a 60-day clock measured in milestones only holds if each milestone has an agreed definition. The tells to watch are physical, not rhetorical: do IAEA inspectors actually arrive this week; does a first tranche of funds move; does the Washington Israel–Lebanon round (June 23–25) keep the airstrike pause alive; and does Brent (~$78) stay calm. If the concrete steps slip while both capitals keep claiming victory, the roadmap becomes a countdown to the next rupture.

📈 The Fed that might hike: the quiet story under the de-escalation rally

the shiftMarkets spent the week cheering cheaper oil — but the more consequential move is monetary. Under new Chair Kevin Warsh, the Federal Reserve has gone from "when do we cut?" to a committee where nine of eighteen officials project at least one hike this year (six see two), markets price a December hike above 50%, and big banks now flag a possible September move. A US central bank tightening into a soft-ish economy is a regime change most portfolios aren't positioned for.

Why a hike is suddenly on the table

  • The Iran war reset the inflation path. The energy spike pushed PCE up to a revised 3.6% and CPI to ~4.2%. Even with crude now falling, the Fed is treating the shock as a reason to guard credibility rather than to look through it.
  • Warsh runs a different playbook. The new chair has leaned hard on "price stability," stripped the easing bias from guidance, and signalled lighter forward guidance overall — a hawkish temperament that markets are still learning to price.
  • The data calendar is the referee. Thursday's PCE is the immediate test; a hot print pulls September-hike odds forward, a soft one buys the doves time.

How it's already showing up in prices

  • Yields up, gold down, dollar firm. The 10-year sits near 4.5%, gold fell ~1.5% to ~$4,129 despite Middle East risk, and the strong dollar is exactly what pins the yen near ¥161.5 — Tokyo can't out-talk a widening rate gap.
  • The rotation tell. Monday's cleanest signal was the Russell 2000's first-ever close at 3,000 even as the Nasdaq fell ~1.3% (Alphabet −5–7%). Money is leaving rate-sensitive mega-cap tech for small caps — a classic late-cycle, higher-for-longer rotation.
  • The oil cross-current. Falling Brent (~$78) should cool inflation into the autumn — the one force that could stay the Fed's hand. The tension between a hawkish Fed and a deflating oil shock is the macro story of the next two months.
Why it matters: the whole 2025 consensus was "rates only go down from here." A Fed openly weighing a hike inverts that — repricing growth stocks, the dollar, EM currencies and the carry trade all at once. Watch Thursday's PCE, whether September-hike language hardens, the 10-year's path toward (or past) 4.5%, and whether the small-cap rotation is a durable trend or a one-day headline. The tail risk runs the other way too: if oil's drop drags inflation down fast, a Fed that has talked itself toward a hike could be caught offside.
