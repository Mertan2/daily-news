1 · The AI trade breaks on good news

What changed this week

For two weeks the AI selloff had a narrative. In early July it was an OpenAI inference-efficiency report; on July 7 it was a Reuters scoop that DeepSeek is building its own inference chip, which sent the KOSPI down 7.6% into a circuit-breaker. Both were stories about the moat — about whether Nvidia and the HBM makers could keep charging what they charge.

This week the narrative stopped being necessary. On July 16, TSMC reported the best quarter in its history:

MetricQ2 2026Change
Net incomeNT$706.56bn (~$22bn)+77.4% y/y — 5th straight record
RevenueNT$1.27tn (US$40.20bn)+36% y/y
2026 capex guidance$60–64bnraised from $52–56bn
Full-year revenue growth"slightly above 40%"guidance raised
US commitment$265bn total+$100bn for Arizona
Share price reaction−4%+the tell

CEO C.C. Wei said AI demand stays strong through 2030 and put another $100bn behind that claim. The market sold it anyway, and the selling propagated everywhere semiconductors trade.

The damage, in one day

  • TokyoNikkei −4.03% to 64,140 — briefly down 4,100+ points intraday, worst level in a month, −6.4% on the week. Tokyo Electron −8.2%, Advantest −7.2%, SoftBank −9%, Taiyo Yuden −11.9%.
  • KioxiaDown as much as 16% Friday, now −52% from its June peak — at least ¥30tn (~$185bn) erased. This is a stock that rose 600% from the start of 2026 and briefly passed Toyota as Japan's most valuable company in mid-June. It is now fourth. A Texas jury separately ordered it to pay Viasat $229m over a memory patent.
  • Mainland ChinaShanghai Composite −1.85% to 3,882.4 and Shenzhen −1.97% to 14,488.7 — both three-month lows. Cambricon −5.06%, NAURA −5.37%, SMIC −1.2%. Hong Kong diverged, Hang Seng +1.4%.
  • USNasdaq and S&P fell as tech turned negative; Micron and the SMH ETF led semis lower.
  • KoreaThe BOK's surprise 25bp hike to 2.75% — its first in 3.5 years — pressured Samsung and SK Hynix and exported the selling into Japanese names.
Why this is a different animal. A moat story is falsifiable: if DeepSeek's chip underdelivers, the trade recovers. What happened this week is multiple compression — the market lowering what it will pay per dollar of AI earnings, while conceding the earnings are real and growing 77%. There is no scoop to disprove, no product to benchmark. That makes it much harder to call a bottom, because nothing has to be wrong for it to continue.

The counterweight: governments are buying the dip at national scale

The same week markets punished AI, Japan committed up to ¥1 trillion (~$6.1bn) over five years to it. Nvidia and Noetra — founded by SoftBank Corp., Sony, NEC and Honda, with 44 investing companies — will build a 140MW AI factory with 27,500 Rubin GPUs and 13,750 Vera CPUs, the compute base for METI's FRONTia physical-AI program (¥387.3bn in year one, via a June 30 NEDO tender with AIST). Japan's AI Robotics Strategy targets 30%+ of the global AI robotics market by 2040, a $133bn prize.

Two readings, and this page does not know which is right:

  • Prescient. State capital is patient in a way public markets are not. If physical AI and robotics are a 2030s market, a five-year program does not care about a July drawdown — and buys compute cheaper for it.
  • Badly timed. Japan is levering into AI at 30-year-high bond yields (10-year JGB at 2.87–2.90%) against a ¥370tn investment strategy the bond market is already disciplining. If AI capex is what compresses, the state bought the top with borrowed money.

Rapidus adds a third data point: President Atsuyoshi Koike said on July 9 it will price 2nm chips at or below TSMC's (benchmarked to ~¥3–3.5m/wafer) ahead of 2027 mass production in Chitose. Everyone is still building.

What would settle it

  • Monday's Asian open. Does Tokyo follow through on −4%, or was Friday a liquidity event?
  • HBM pricing and hyperscaler capex guidance. The cleanest read on whether demand or the multiple is moving.
  • Whether TSMC's guidance survives contact. "Slightly above 40%" growth and $60–64bn capex is a falsifiable claim with a date on it.
  • DeepSeek's raise. Reportedly seeking ≥RMB 480bn (~$71bn) pre-money with an IPO possibly filing this year. If a company can raise at $71bn while the trade sells off, private and public markets have decoupled on AI.

2 · Iran — from a shredded ceasefire to a named red line

Where this thread stood

The July 6 edition of this page asked one question about Iran: who succeeds Khamenei, killed at the war's outset on February 28, whose seven-day state funeral (July 3–9) ended with presumed successor Mojtaba Khamenei still not appearing in public. That question is still open — and the war has moved on without answering it.

The escalation ladder, two weeks

  • Jul 7Iran's IRGC fired missiles at commercial ships in the Strait of Hormuz, including the Qatari LNG tanker Al-Rekayyat — significant damage, no casualties. The US revoked the license authorizing Iranian oil sales; Brent spiked toward $76.
  • Jul 8US Central Command struck 80+ targets — air defenses, C2, coastal radar, anti-ship missiles and 60+ IRGC boats in and near the strait. Declared complete.
  • Jul 15Turkish reporting says the ceasefire — the "Ilamabad Accord" — collapsed before its term expired. CENTCOM announced the latest wave of strikes concluded at 21:00.
  • Jul 16The US expanded strikes northward toward Tehran and fired on a ship said to be breaching the naval blockade. Iran reported 35+ killed, 300+ wounded. Targets: missile production and space facilities in Semnan, Greater Tunb Island, bridges and a railway junction near Bandar Abbas, a barracks in Sistan and Baluchestan. Iran retaliated against US allies in the region. Trump said a peace deal remained possible.
IRGC spokesman Col. Ebrahim Zolfaghari, on the Strait of Hormuz: "This is Iran's invincible red line." Iran has already fired on shipping there once this month. The question is whether a stated red line is a threat or a description of what it has decided to do.

The strange part: oil is not panicking

On July 16, as strikes reached toward Tehran, WTI slipped 0.21% to $79.43 and Brent fell 0.49% to $84.53. Elevated, near recent highs — but falling, on the day the campaign escalated. Either the market believes the strait stays open, or it believes the blockade is doing its work, or it is not pricing the tail. Whichever it is, this is the cheapest available signal that the consensus expects containment, and it is the first thing that would break if that's wrong.

Turkey is the exposed NATO member

Hürriyet Daily News led with "US strikes Iran as Tehran warns of Mideast reprisals" and "US-Israel rift widens as Vance steps up criticism." Ankara's position is uncomfortable by geography: a NATO member bordering the war, hosting a COP presidency and a Cyprus process, with a currency at 47.05/$ and 32.11% inflation that an oil shock would worsen directly.

Unconfirmed: Reporting that Turkey's MFA re-summoned Iran's ambassador Mohammad Hasan Habibollahzadeh, and that Fidan warned Iran to direct no further missiles at Turkey, reached this page via aggregator summaries — not a primary MFA statement. The strikes and ceasefire collapse are corroborated by Hürriyet Daily News and Türkiye Gazetesi; the Turkey-specific diplomatic detail is not treated as established. A separate claim that Türkiye will take the naval component of Ukraine security guarantees appeared only on a Russian-linked aggregator and is likewise unconfirmed.

What decides the next two weeks

  • Hormuz. Does Iran act on the red line? A single successful strike on a tanker reprices global energy and, through it, every central bank decision on this site's calendar.
  • Succession. Mojtaba's continued absence is now five months old. A regime that cannot show its leader may not be able to make a durable deal — or to climb down.
  • Whether "a deal is possible" means anything. Trump says so while the campaign expands. Nuclear talks have been paused since early July.
  • The US–Israel rift. Flagged by Turkish front pages as widening, with Vance escalating criticism. An underreported variable in Western coverage.
Sources: PBS NewsHour · Hürriyet Daily News · Türkiye Gazetesi · TheStreet (oil)

3 · AI governance splits into blocs — in 24 hours

Two announcements, one day apart, pointing opposite ways

This is new to these pages, and it earned its place: on July 16 and 17 the world produced two incompatible institutional answers to "who governs AI," neither of which acknowledges the other.

ShanghaiThe World AI Cooperation Organization

On July 16, 29 countries signed the founding agreement for WAICO — a genuine intergovernmental body, headquartered in Shanghai. Signatories include Russia, Belarus, Serbia, Cuba, Brazil and Venezuela, plus 10 African and 12 Asian states. Absent: the United States and every major EU economy.

On July 17, Xi gave his first-ever keynote at the World AI Conference. The content is worth reading precisely:

  • China would "encourage open source, openness, collaboration and sharing."
  • Governance should be "people-centered" and stay "secure and controllable."
  • A warning against any country putting its own security above others'.
  • 5,000 AI training and seminar places for developing countries over five years; cooperation frameworks with ASEAN and the League of Arab States.

The offer is coherent and, to a middle-income country, attractive: open weights, training, no lectures, a seat at a table that exists. "Secure and controllable" is the load-bearing phrase — it is simultaneously a safety claim and a sovereignty claim, and it is doing a lot of work.

BrusselsThe DMA turns on Google

On July 16 the Commission issued two binding DMA specification decisions requiring Google to:

  • Give third-party AI assistants the same Android access Gemini enjoys — including voice activation and in-app actions like booking a taxi.
  • Share anonymised ranking, query, click and view data from Google Search with rival search engines and AI chatbots, beginning by January 2027, with a formula set for pricing that data.

Google's objection — that this "risks undermining vital privacy and security guardrails for millions of Europeans" — is the mirror image of Beijing's "secure and controllable": both invoke safety as grounds for control. Europe's theory is that competition is the safeguard and incumbency the risk. It is the most consequential DMA enforcement yet and a template for regulating AI distribution rather than AI models.

The asymmetry. China is building an institution and inviting members. Europe is writing rules and enforcing them on a company. The US is doing neither this week — it is capping Chinese journalists' visas at 90 days and alleging election theft. One of these is a strategy for the countries that haven't chosen yet.

The evidence that this isn't abstract

A Meta Oversight Board study released July 16 tested 10 commercial LLMs from Meta, Anthropic, OpenAI and others, and found them significantly more likely to refuse generating political criticism of restrictive leaders — Thailand's king, Saudi Arabia's crown prince, China's leader — than of permissive governments.

  • Claude declined to criticize Thailand's king and Saudi Arabia's crown prince, but complied for Trump and King Charles III.
  • ChatGPT gave different answers about Chinese democracy depending on whether it was prompted in English or Chinese.

The Board's warning: developers risk building "AI infrastructure that... has the effect of extending illegitimate restrictions on freedom of expression globally." No treaty required — the models already encode a geography of what can be said. Whoever sets the defaults sets the speech norms, which is exactly what both WAICO and the DMA are arguing about.

The adjacent fact that makes it concrete

On July 15 the Cyberspace Administration of China cleared seven on-device AI models for the first time — Apple Intelligence, Samsung Galaxy AI, Huawei, Xiaomi, Oppo, Vivo, ZTE's Nubia. To ship in China, Apple Intelligence runs on Alibaba's Tongyi Qwen, not OpenAI or Google. The partnership was disclosed in February 2025 and only now cleared. That is what jurisdictional AI looks like in practice: the same phone, a different mind, depending on the border.

What to watch

  • Which middle powers join WAICO. The founding 29 are largely aligned already. The signal is the 30th through 50th — an India, an Indonesia, a Brazil-adjacent swing state.
  • Whether Google litigates or complies by the January 2027 data-sharing deadline. Compliance sets a template; litigation sets a timeline.
  • Whether the US builds any counter-forum — or continues to treat AI diplomacy as a trade-restriction question.
  • Pew's finding as backdrop: China now polls more favorably than the US in 25 of 36 countries, with median confidence in Xi 10 points above Trump. WAICO is being offered into that.
Attribution: WAICO's framing as broad global buy-in is Chinese state-media emphasis; the 29-member list is independently confirmed by Reuters. Western outlets frame WAICO explicitly as a counter-bloc. The Pew data is independent, though Chinese state media amplified it heavily — treat the framing, not the numbers, as the state-media layer.
