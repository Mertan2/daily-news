1 · The AI-chip map is being redrawn — and not in Silicon Valley's favor

The concession that mattered

The single most consequential tech line this week came from Nvidia CEO Jensen Huang, who said the company has "largely lost" China's AI-chip market. The numbers behind it are stark: Nvidia's share of Chinese AI accelerators is now seen falling to roughly 8% in 2026, while Huawei's climbs toward ~50%. Two years of tightening US export controls were meant to slow China down; instead they handed Huawei a captive domestic market and a reason to build at scale.

Three shifts hiding inside one story

  • Self-sufficiency compoundsExport controls are a one-time tax on the target, but a permanent subsidy to its domestic champions. Huawei didn't get better because it out-innovated Nvidia; it got better because it was the only option left in a market of that size.
  • Memory > logicSK Hynix passing Samsung is the tell that the scarcest input in AI right now is high-bandwidth memory (HBM), not the GPU die itself. Whoever controls HBM supply controls the pace of the buildout — and that leverage now sits in Korea.
  • Sovereignty as strategyJapan's Noetra and Rapidus, Korea's ~$518B fab plan, and China's domestic-compute grid are all versions of the same bet: that AI capacity is now infrastructure a serious country builds at home rather than rents from abroad.

So what: the AI trade is usually told as a Nvidia-and-hyperscalers story. This week reframed it as a geography story — capacity fragmenting into national blocs, with Chinese self-sufficiency accelerating precisely because it was walled off, and the real bottleneck (HBM) sitting in Korea rather than California.

2 · The "One Big Beautiful Bill" — what actually passed

The vote

On July 3 the House gave final approval to H.R.1 by 218–214, with only two Republicans — Thomas Massie (KY) and Brian Fitzpatrick (PA) — joining every Democrat against it. The Senate had cleared it two days earlier 51–50, with Vice-President JD Vance breaking the tie and three Republicans (Paul, Tillis, Collins) voting no. Trump is expected to sign it at a July 4 event — making it the defining domestic law of the term.

What's in it

  • TaxesMakes the 2017 tax cuts permanent and adds a temporary "no tax on tips" provision (2025–2028).
  • HealthCBO scores roughly $930B in Medicaid cuts and estimates ~11.8M more people uninsured by 2034 — the most contested piece of the package.
  • DeficitMaking the cuts permanent removes the revenue the 2017 law's sunsets were supposed to recover, so the long-run deficit path widens.

Why the timing cuts against it

The bill lands in the same 48 hours as a labor market that just missed badly — +57K June payrolls versus ~115K expected, with prior months revised down 74K. A permanent tax cut is a bet on growth; a cooling jobs market and a Fed that has stopped talking about rate cuts (and lifted its 2026 core-inflation projection to ~3.3%) is exactly the backdrop in which a deficit-widening law draws the most scrutiny from bond markets.

  • The coverage cliffMedicaid changes phase in over years, so the political pain (and the ~11.8M figure) arrives on a delay — much of it after the 2026 midterms but during the campaign for them.
  • Near-party-linePassing 218–214 with two GOP defectors means there's no bipartisan cover; every consequence, good or bad, now belongs to one party.

So what: H.R.1 is genuinely big — permanent tax cuts, a large Medicaid rollback, a wider deficit — and it passed with almost no margin and no crossover votes. Its fate as policy and as politics will be decided by two things it can't control: whether growth holds up as the labor market cools, and how voters feel the coverage losses as they phase in.

3 · The arithmetic of Kyiv's deadliest night

What happened overnight July 1–2

Russia launched roughly 570 aerial weapons at Ukraine in a single night — about 74 missiles (including Zircon and Iskander ballistic types) and ~496 Shahed-type drones. Ukraine says it downed 524 of them. But the ~9% that got through was enough: part of a nine-storey residential building in Kyiv's Darnytskyi district collapsed, and the death toll rose to 30 by July 3, with more than 100 injured. Early counts ran 22–27 before climbing — a reminder to treat first-night tolls as provisional.

The defender's math problem

A 92% interception rate sounds like a win, and tactically it is. But air defense is an economics problem, not just a physics one. A Shahed drone costs tens of thousands of dollars; the interceptors that reliably stop ballistic missiles cost orders of magnitude more. When the attacker sends 570 objects in one wave, the goal isn't to get most of them through — it's to saturate the defense, force it to expend scarce high-end interceptors on cheap drones, and let the handful of leak-throughs do the work. The 8% that got past is the whole point of the 100%.

  • Saturation logicMass matters more than accuracy. Cheap drones exist to soak up interceptors so the expensive missiles arrive against a depleted defense.
  • Grid pressureUkraine reimposed nationwide rolling blackouts July 1 as extreme heat pushed demand up 25%+ — energy infrastructure is a parallel target to the cities themselves.
  • The interceptor questionThe strategic worry for Kyiv and its backers isn't any one night; it's whether the supply of high-end interceptors can keep pace with barrages this size, sustained.

So what: "524 of 570 shot down" reads like a defensive triumph, but the barrage is designed so that even a 92% success rate leaves a lethal remainder. The real contest isn't interception rate — it's the cost-exchange ratio and whether interceptor stockpiles can outlast the drone assembly lines.
