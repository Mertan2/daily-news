Lead14.9%: the interceptor ledger stops being an inference

The numberUkraine's Defence Ministry stated that Ukrainian air defence intercepted 29 of 195 Russian ballistic missiles during July, and that it "operated in July under conditions of a critical shortage of interceptor missiles." That is a 14.9% success rate against ballistics for a full calendar month.

Why this particular number matters. Since Tuesday this page has been tracking a single mechanism: the United States has expended "virtually all" of its ATACMS and Precision Strike Missile stocks, roughly 80% of THAAD interceptors and about half its Patriot inventory across five months of the Iran campaign, and CENTCOM has been pulling from other theatres to reload. Kyiv and Tel Aviv draw on overlapping stock. Until now the Ukrainian side of that was visible only as individual bad nights — 24 ballistics on Wednesday with none stopped, one of 27 in late July. A monthly figure is a different kind of evidence: it removes the possibility that the zero-interception nights were flukes of targeting or weather, and it puts the degradation on a trend line the Ukrainian government is willing to publish.

Context for scale: through 2024 and much of 2025, Ukrainian ballistic interception rates were reported in ranges from roughly 40% to occasionally above 80% when Patriot coverage was dense. Whatever the precise historical baseline, a 14.9% month is a different regime, and the ministry has named the cause rather than obscuring it.

OvernightThe pattern continued into Saturday. A Russian attack on the Brovary district of Kyiv Oblast killed three people, including a child, and injured seven. Ukraine's General Staff put cumulative Russian losses at 1,456,610 since February 2022, including 1,190 in the past day — a figure from one belligerent and not independently verifiable, cited here as a claim.one-source claim Monitoring channels reported Ukrainian strikes overnight on oil refineries in Samara Oblast and Krasnodar Krai.

Friday's damage report is the other half of the winter problem: Russia struck seven Naftogaz facilities in eastern Ukraine, destroying critical equipment and forcing multiple sites offline — gas production infrastructure, hit in August, ahead of a heating season. One of Ukraine's largest football stadiums, in Odesa, was also damaged.

United StatesAn 86–11 sanctions vote, then a five-week recess

SenateThe Senate passed the Lindsey O. Graham Sanctioning Russia and Iran Act of 2026 by 86 votes to 11. The bill authorises the President to impose tariffs of up to 100% on the top five purchasers of Russian oil or natural gas — in practice China and India first — extends Iran sanctions, targets Putin personally along with officials, oligarchs and Russian banks, and goes after the shadow tanker networks used to evade existing embargoes. Countries importing less than 15% of their gas from Russia and taking "significant" steps to cut further are exempt.

The catch is the calendar. The bill now goes to the House, which is in summer recess; a vote is not expected before early September. Democratic Representatives Gregory Meeks and Don Beyer have already called the House version "unacceptable," objecting to granting effectively unrestricted tariff authority. So the Senate has passed a instrument of enormous leverage and then made it unavailable for a month.

The bill is named for Senator Lindsey Graham, who championed it for years and secured White House sign-off shortly before his death on 11 July. His sister, Darline Graham Nordone, now filling the seat, said it "hits Putin where it hurts." Senator Jim Risch: "This could finally bring Russia to the negotiating table and end this conflict between Russia and Ukraine." President Zelensky called American pressure and sanctions "what will help the most"; Ursula von der Leyen also welcomed it.

Sources: Al Jazeera, Euronews, RFE/RL, CBS News, Bloomberg.

TürkiyeSeventeen and a half hours, one committee, one signature short of a floor vote

Passed committeeThe Milli Dayanışma ve Toplumsal Bütünleşmenin Güçlendirilmesine Dair Kanun Teklifi — the framework law of the "Terörsüz Türkiye" process — was approved by the TBMM Justice Commission in the early hours of Saturday, after a sitting that ran roughly 17½ hours (some outlets say 18). It goes to the General Assembly tomorrow, Sunday 9 August.

New detail from the committee text. The bill establishes a special board chaired by the Vice President, comprising the Ministers of Justice, Foreign Affairs, Interior and National Defence, the Presidential Chief of Staff, the head of MİT and the Secretary-General of the National Security Council. That is a considerably heavier institutional apparatus than the twelve articles implied when the bill was tabled on Wednesday, and it centralises supervision of the disarmament process in the executive.

Signature count moved. The bill was submitted with 360 signatures on Wednesday; the pro-government press reported 367 by the time it reached committee.figure moving Either way it spans AK Parti (277) and MHP (46) plus DEM Parti, CHP and HÜDA PAR members — out of 592 seats.

The opposition that showed up. İYİ Parti opposed the measure, refused to join the related commissions, and protested the late opening of the session by knocking on the committee-room door. The commission chair pushed back on the "amnesty" framing, stressing that the law defers rather than cancels sentences. DEM Parti proposed an amendment to Article 4.

What still has not happened. Nothing in the law takes effect until the MGK formally determines that all PKK/KCK-linked structures have dissolved and surrendered weapons, and that determination is published in the Official Gazette. Only then does a six-month application window open. Parliament can pass this tomorrow and produce no legal effect for months. The floor vote is the visible step; the MGK certification is the decision.

Sources: Ekonomim, Sabah, BirGün, Yeni Asır, Yetkin Report, Gazete Oksijen, TBMM records.

Middle EastThe pact gets its first analysts, and Baghdad gets its invitation back

Mecca pactA day after signature, the first substantive analysis of the Mecca Joint Defence Agreement has landed, and it is more measured than the "Sunni NATO" headlines. Three readings worth holding together:

  • Kristian Coates Ulrichsen (Rice University): regional states "have harbored growing doubts about the reliability of the US security partnership" and are moving to "diversify security and defence relations."
  • Sina Toossi (Center for International Policy): "The regional order is being reshaped by the US–Israeli war on Iran."
  • Yasmine Farouk (International Crisis Group), supplying the limit: "These countries are all US partners, and they recognise that there is no framework that can replace the United States."

The consensus reading is supplement, not substitute — a hedge built by three governments that were frustrated at how little they were consulted before the Iran war began, and that then watched Washington struggle to supply air defence. All three signatories have publicly called for mediation and de-escalation with Iran, which is not the posture of an alliance forming to fight one.

Reactions, one day on. India's MEA spokesman Randhir Jaiswal said New Delhi is "closely following" the development. Iranian state media had characterised the pact's 2025 Saudi–Pakistan predecessor as non-threatening and possibly a step toward a broader regional framework — no comparable statement on this one yet. The Trump administration had not commented at time of writing. Israeli coverage is framing it as an Article-5-style guarantee that raises questions for both Israel and Iran.

HormuzNo joint statement yet. The Iran–Oman technical track has agreement on coordinates: inbound traffic routed through a northern corridor near the Iranian coast, outbound through a southern corridor along the Omani coast — a reversal of the decades-old arrangement in which commercial traffic ran through Omani waters. Iran's Esmail Baghaei says the joint statement covers "technical, legal, security and environmental" points and is in final drafting; the corridor would run two to four months initially. An Iranian official framed the condition plainly: a deal is struck "if third parties don't obstruct the process."

The separate document remains the problem. The Strategic Action for the Security and Sustainable Development of the Strait of Hormuz and the Persian Gulf bill, before the Majlis National Security Committee, would bar US-, Israeli- and other "hostile"-linked vessels outright, levy tolls up to 7% of cargo value and fine violators 20%. Washington has rejected it. Lawmaker Hosseinali Hajideligani, first deputy chair of parliament's Article 90 committee, confirms documentation is being compiled for FM Araghchi's impeachment — the stated grievance being failure to force US acceptance of maximalist terms: full Iranian control of the strait, Israeli withdrawal from Lebanon, US withdrawal from the region. Another lawmaker, Morteza Mahmoudi, said Araghchi would already have faced impeachment over a Hormuz reopening post were it not for wartime considerations, accusing him of remarks that "calm global oil and energy markets."

That last accusation is the clearest statement anyone has made of the actual disagreement: one faction in Tehran regards calming the oil market as the offence.

The one hard transit number this week came from India, not from either negotiating party: the shipping ministry reported more than 4,000 seafarers repatriated and 62 Indian vessels safely transited the strait. Brent has been trading in the $79–82 band on the technical track alone.

Japan & Asia-PacificNagasaki tomorrow, with the wording still unresolved

TomorrowNagasaki marks the 81st anniversary of its bombing on Sunday 9 August, and it arrives with Thursday's question still open. At Hiroshima, PM Sanae Takaichi said Japan "is upholding" the Three Non-Nuclear Principles — present tense, describing a state of affairs — rather than pledging to maintain them, as Ishiba and Kishida each did explicitly from the same podium. Asked directly, she said: "It's premature to comment on the final form of the wording as we are in the middle of work to revise the documents before year-end," adding that Japan "firmly upholds the three non-nuclear principles in the direction of its policy."

Note that international coverage split on how to characterise this: The Japan Times headlined it as Takaichi emphasising commitment; SCMP, CCTV and others as stopping short of a pledge. Both are defensible readings of the same two sentences, which is precisely what makes the formulation notable — it was built to be readable either way.framing varies by outlet

Takaichi met hibakusha representatives, who raised concern over a possible review. The principles are cabinet policy, not statute; the security-document revision due before end-2026 is legally sufficient to alter them. Reported polling puts support for keeping them above two-thirds. Watch tomorrow's Nagasaki language for whether the verb changes back.

YenAn important clarification on the intervention story, because two separate operations have been getting conflated. The 31 July action was a joint US–Japan yen purchase — confirmed by both sides on 3 August, the first coordinated US–Japan yen-buying since 1998 and the first joint intervention of any kind since the G7 acted after the 2011 earthquake. It moved the yen from about ¥164 to ¥156.9, and both governments said they were ready to repeat it. Thursday 6 August's operation, estimated by Bloomberg from BOJ accounts at roughly $53bn (¥8.45trn), appears to be a separate and largely unilateral follow-up — likely the largest single-day intervention on record, producing a 3.3% intraday move, of which nearly half had been surrendered by Friday morning at 158.45.reconstructed from multiple reports

The significance of US participation is worth stating plainly: Washington's motive is not Japanese competitiveness but its own bond market. A Japan forced into large-scale reserve liquidation becomes a forced seller of Treasuries. That is why analysts read the joint operation as potentially advancing the BOJ's next tightening move — the intervention only works if the rate differential eventually narrows. Against that: a 10-year JGB at 2.50%, the highest since July 1997, debt near 237% of GDP, an approved consumption-tax cut on food from 8% to 1% for two years from April 2027, and a prime minister who asked the BOJ Governor in May to buy JGBs to cap long yields.

Sources: CNBC, Wolf Street, Central Banking, Bloomberg, MoF/BOJ accounts.

EuropeA record fire season, a suspended Schengen, and 400 people in tents

FiresEU burned area reached 500,852 hectares as of 6 August — past both the same-date 2025 figure (the previous record year) and the 20-year average. By 4 August the season had killed at least 17 civilians, six firefighters and two helicopter crew and driven roughly 330,000 people from their homes in Spain and France alone. June 2026 was Western Europe's hottest June on record at 3.05°C above normal, with anomalies peaking near +9°C in France and Germany and a high of 45.1°C in southern Spain. The UK recorded its driest July in nearly 200 years; all of Wales and more than half of England are officially in drought.

The counter-example still deserves attention: Türkiye is running about 52% below its own 20-year average at roughly 25,700 hectares. Same climate, same latitude band, radically different outcome — a suppression-capacity story rather than a weather one.

Sources: EFFIS / JRC, Copernicus, Al Jazeera, France 24, Bloomberg, OGM.

CeutaThe death toll from the border surge that began 30 July is now confirmed by Ceuta's leader at at least 100 — 72 on the Spanish side plus 11 reported by Morocco in earlier counts, with the total revised upward since. An estimated 80,000 people crossed; between 3,500 and 5,000 undocumented migrants remain in the enclave. Many drowned; others died in the crush at the Tarajal breakwater.

The European consequence has outrun the event. Italy suspended Schengen free movement with Spain, closing air and sea borders to "targeted and selective" checks on non-EU travellers; FM Antonio Tajani called it "a necessary choice… made unavoidable today." Twenty-two member states signed a joint letter aligning with Rome; France strengthened controls on its land border with Spain. PM Pedro Sánchez's reply: "Solidarity and empathy are optional. Respect for European treaties and the facts is not," citing Frontex data showing Italy recorded more irregular entries than Spain from 2021 to 2026. Commissioner Magnus Brunner, meanwhile, relayed Spanish FM José Manuel Albares's account that "practically all" who entered Ceuta have returned and "not a single person has crossed to mainland EU."

So: an internal Schengen border was suspended over a movement that, on the Commission's own reported figures, did not reach mainland Europe. That gap between the measure and the movement is the story.

Sources: Al Jazeera, Euronews, The Hill, NPR, CNN, Frontex.

EbolaThe DRC's Bundibugyo outbreak stands at 3,973 confirmed cases and 1,801 deaths as of 5 August (data to 4 August), with 674 patients hospitalised in isolation — up from 3,874 / 1,751 four days earlier and 3,605 / 1,587 four weeks earlier. SpaceAstronomers reported catching an X-ray shock breakout from a broad-lined Type Ic supernova — EP260321a / SN 2026gzf, about 500 million light-years away — capturing a massive star's death from the first explosive moment, a notoriously hard observation to time.
