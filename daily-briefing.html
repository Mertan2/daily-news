Lead storyThe Iran war paused itself again — mid-attack

Trump cancels the strike package, claims a framework

President Trump announced late Saturday that he had called off a pending attack on Iran, saying a rough framework of a deal had been reached and that Iran "and other Middle Eastern countries" had asked him to hold off. He described the terms as an "immediate, complete, and total" reopening of the Strait of Hormuz plus an end to Iran's nuclear programme — conditional, he said, on the deal being closed quickly.

Nothing about the surrounding day supported the word "ceasefire." Kuwait's army said it had been intercepting Iranian drones since dawn Saturday, aimed at "vital" installations including a government facility in the north of the country; falling debris caused material damage but no casualties. The State Department issued warnings urging Americans across the region to leave. Trump separately threatened further strikes if talks stall.

What to make of it: this is the second pause in nine days. The first — four quiet nights the week of 27 July — ended when the IRGC hit US forces in Jordan and CENTCOM answered with a two-hour barrage. A pause announced by one side while the other is still flying drones is a bargaining position, not a state of affairs. The honest indicator remains Hormuz transit counts, not statements.

Sources: CNN live · CBS News · PBS NewsHour · The National · Anadolu

The constraint nobody announced: America is short of destroyers

Gen. Alexus Grynkewich, head of US European Command, sent written notification to senior Pentagon officials this week saying that without another Navy destroyer he will be forced to choose defence of the US homeland over defence of Israel against Iranian ballistic missiles.

The arithmetic: five destroyers deploy from Rota, Spain, with a sixth due later this year. As of Friday, two were in the Mediterranean and three were alongside in port. Five months of war have driven up maintenance demand and burned through interceptor inventory.

Why this matters more than the deal talk: for weeks the ceiling on this war has been described in political terms. This is the first explicit statement from a combatant commander that it is a materiel ceiling. Munitions stocks and hull availability bound the tempo more concretely than any negotiating framework does — and they bound the American side, not the Iranian one.

Sources: Washington Post · Haaretz

Gaza: two governments describing two different agreements

The Board of Peace published a 15-point roadmap calling on Israel and Hamas to fully implement the Sharm el-Sheikh agreement, under which Gaza's new technocratic administration would oversee decommissioning and storage of heavy weapons, military facilities and tunnels.

  • Hamas (official Ghazi Hamad) confirmed an agreement to disarm — but only after Israel ceases fire, stops targeted killings, withdraws to the "Yellow Line," and permits an aid surge consistent with the October ceasefire.
  • Israel rejected key elements: an Israeli official said complete disarmament remains a precondition for any process, and there will be no withdrawal from the Yellow Line until demilitarisation is finished. UN ambassador Danny Danon said "cosmetic solutions" will not work.
  • Israel stayed conspicuously silent on Friday when Washington announced the roadmap — the tell.
  • Three Palestinians were killed in Gaza City. Israeli forces killed Hezbollah fighters in southern Lebanon despite the ceasefire framework there.

Sequencing is the whole dispute: Hamas wants withdrawal first, Israel wants disarmament first, and the roadmap does not resolve which comes first. That is the same gap that has survived every announcement since October.

Michigan joins Minnesota: water systems breached, FBI investigating

  • Michigan reported cyberattacks on nine of the state's water systems on Saturday; an official said all systems were operating "safely."
  • Earlier in the week, authorities said more than 30 Minnesota water systems had been targeted.
  • The FBI said Saturday it is investigating both. It has not publicly named a culprit.

Read this next to the destroyer story. Both are the same war measured on the American side of the ocean: one in hulls, one in PLCs. Attribution is not confirmed and should not be assumed — but the advisory came first and the incidents followed.

EuropeCeuta becomes an EU-level crisis; Kyiv runs out of interceptors

Ceuta: 67 dead, ~69,500 returned, 22 leaders demanding a summit

The border between Morocco and Spain's North African enclave of Ceuta collapsed on Thursday night and through Friday. The death toll has climbed to 67 — some drowned, others killed in a stampede across a breakwater barrier. Approximately 69,500 people returned to Morocco from Ceuta within 30 hours after the mass crossings; estimates of how many entered run to around 60,000. There were parallel attempts at Melilla, where migrants clashed with Moroccan police in Bni Nsar, throwing rocks and setting police vehicles alight.

On Saturday, Italy's Giorgia Meloni and Denmark's Mette Frederiksen led 22 EU heads of state and government in signing a letter demanding an emergency videoconference of interior ministers. From the letter: "We cannot allow uncontrolled mass crossings, the instrumentalisation of migration or other hybrid threats to create the perception that illegal entry into the European Union is possible." Ireland, holding the rotating presidency, said the meeting will take place Tuesday.

The detail that will matter: France, Portugal and Luxembourg did not sign. The letter is therefore not a European position — it is a bloc within Europe formalising itself, with Spain's own government among the signatories asking for help.

Context: Spain's Supreme Court ruled on 29 June that summary return procedures cannot be applied to migrants intercepted trying to reach Ceuta or Melilla by sea, requiring ordinary immigration procedures instead. Local workers' associations describe thousands sleeping in parks and on pavements, including unaccompanied children.

Sources: Euronews · NPR · Al Jazeera · Radio Prague · Wikipedia

Kyiv: 27 ballistic missiles, one interception

  • Overnight into Saturday, Russia fired 35 missiles at Ukraine — 27 of them ballistic — plus 185 drones.
  • Nine people were killed in Kyiv and 27–30 injured, including four children. (The Moscow Times reported at least ten dead.)
  • Darnytskyi district: seven killed, 14 injured. Solomianskyi: two killed, eight injured when debris hit a five-storey residential building. Eighteen apartment buildings and private homes, two educational institutions, shops, a clinic and an administrative building were damaged.
  • Only one ballistic missile was intercepted — Zelensky said flatly that there are no interceptors left for the Patriot system.
  • It was the second major attack in 48 hours; Thursday's barrage killed at least ten across Ukraine and wounded more than 50, and included the Kh-101 that detonated on Polish soil near Lublin without triggering an Article 4 consultation.
  • Separately, Ukrainian forces sank a Rosatom-owned container ship in the Black Sea, forcing its 17-member crew to abandon it. Rosatom said it was carrying civilian goods including frozen food and construction materials.

The structural story: "nine killed in Kyiv" is a headline that has run many times. "One of 27 intercepted" has not. Ukraine's ballistic-missile defence depends on a US-supplied interceptor stock that is now being drawn down simultaneously by the Iran theatre. The Kyiv casualty count and the Grynkewich memo are the same shortage seen from two ends.

China & the Indo-PacificThe export leg weakens before the consumption leg is fixed

No stimulus is coming — that is now the confirmed position

  • Following Thursday's Politburo meeting, economists confirmed over the weekend that there was no major announcement: Beijing is sticking to implementing existing policy tools rather than launching a package. The pivot is toward stability, risk management and supply-side growth, not broad demand stimulus.
  • July manufacturing PMI came in at 49.2, down from 50.3 and missing the 50.0 line — ending four months of expansion. New orders fell to 48.5, the lowest since 2023.
  • July export growth slowed to 11.2% y/y from 17.3%. Q2 GDP was 4.3% — the slowest in more than three years, and short of Beijing's own target.
  • The 15th Five-Year Plan (2026–30) targets a "core digital economy" at 12.5% of GDP by 2030 and non-fossil fuels at 25% of energy consumption. Coal fell below 50% of electricity output for the first time in H1, at 49.7%.

The sequencing problem is stark: the export engine that carried the first half is decelerating, and domestic consumption has not been repaired to take the load. Markets are pricing an easing window near the end of Q3. If it slips, the 4.5% floor of the annual target is genuinely in play.

Sources: CNBC · Al Jazeera · SCMP · Asia Pacific Foundation

Sources: Japan Times · NPR · Wikipedia

The yen: the biggest single-day intervention ever bought about half a yen

  • The yen hit a fresh 40-year low around ¥163.99 before recovering to roughly ¥163.57 — after what reporting describes as potentially the largest single-day intervention in the history of Japan's currency market.
  • The BOJ held at 1% on Friday, with Hajime Takata dissenting for a hike. Commentary characterises the bank as hawkish and describes the stance as raising the political stakes for the government.
  • Takaichi's approval has fallen from above 70% at its peak to 57% in July (Yomiuri, from 69% in June); a Jiji poll dated 16 July put her cabinet at 49% — below the 50% threshold for the first time.
  • She has declined to treat intervention as the answer, arguing on 27 July that restoring growth and competitiveness is what will strengthen confidence in the yen, and that exchange rates are shaped by more than government policy.
  • Her fiscal answer: cutting the consumption tax on food and soft drinks to 1% from 8% for two years from April 2027 — a first for Japan. Japan is also standing up a National Intelligence Bureau.
  • Japan's finance minister said US Treasury Secretary Bessent shares concerns over the weak yen.

The trap is circular and well described: a weak yen imports inflation, inflation costs her approval, and low approval weakens the mandate for the structural reform that would fix the yen. Intervention spends reserves to buy time inside that loop without exiting it.

Bundibugyo Ebola in the DRC: 3,605 cases, 1,587 deaths

As ofConfirmed casesDeaths

  • Declared a Public Health Emergency of International Concern on 16 May, ten weeks ago. It is now the fastest-growing Ebola outbreak on record.
  • The species is Bundibugyo ebolavirus. Both licensed vaccines — Ervebo and the Mvabea/Zabdeno regimen — target Zaire, and WHO does not consider them effective here. There is no approved vaccine or specific treatment. The tools are isolation, contact tracing and supportive care.
  • Trials started 12 July in the DRC for remdesivir and MBP134; Oxford has been cleared for a Phase 1 vaccine trial. A study of cross-reactive Bundibugyo antibody responses following licensed Ebola vaccination was published in the NEJM on 22 July.
  • Uganda has reported 20 confirmed cases and two deaths plus one probable fatal case. France confirmed an imported case in a doctor returning from the DRC on 24 June; a US citizen was evacuated to Germany in May. The CDC has renewed travel restrictions through mid-August.

The case fatality ratio is running around 44% and the curve has roughly doubled in a month. Two exported cases have already reached Europe. This is the single largest gap between severity and coverage on this page.

Broad Peak: Nirmal Purja and nine others confirmed dead

Nirmal Purja, 43 — the Nepali climber who set the record for the fastest ascent of all fourteen 8,000-metre peaks, completing it in six months in 2019 — died with nine other members of his expedition in an avalanche on Broad Peak, the world's twelfth-highest mountain at 26,414 feet, in Pakistan's Gilgit-Baltistan region.
