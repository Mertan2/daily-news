LeadThe Mecca pact: a new security architecture, signed in the holiest city

SignedSaudi Crown Prince Mohammed bin Salman, Turkish President Recep Tayyip Erdoğan and Pakistani Prime Minister Shehbaz Sharif signed the Mecca Joint Defence Agreement today at Al-Safa Palace in Mecca. The operative clause, as stated by Pakistan's Foreign Ministry: an armed attack on any one of the three "would be regarded as an attack against them all." Erdoğan and Sharif travelled at the invitation of King Salman; Erdoğan went to Mecca by road after arriving in the Kingdom.

The three governments describe it as purely defensive, open to accession by other regional states, not directed at any specific country, and not abrogating or replacing any existing bilateral or multilateral arrangement. Turkish outlets are reporting it under the shorter name "Mekke Anlaşması" (the Mecca Agreement) or "Mekke Paktı."

Why it is more than a communiqué. The combination is unusual: Türkiye brings NATO's second-largest army and a defence-export industry; Pakistan is the only nuclear-armed state in the Muslim world; Saudi Arabia brings the money, the oil and — by signing in Mecca — a religious legitimacy that no treaty text can manufacture. Analysts quoted around the signing framed it as merging "deterrence and diplomacy" and as the outline of a different regional security architecture, one in which Gulf security is not solely a US franchise.

The timing is not subtle. Negotiations reportedly ran close to a year, but the signature lands (i) one day after Saudi officials warned that coordinated attacks by the Houthis and Iran-aligned Iraqi militias on Saudi ports, airports and energy infrastructure were imminent; (ii) after repeated Iranian and Houthi attacks on the Kingdom dating to late February; and (iii) in the middle of the US–Iran war and the Hormuz closure. A treaty that is formally aimed at no one is, in practice, being read across the region as aimed at exactly one country.

What to watch. Whether the text is published in full; whether "armed attack" is defined to include drone and missile strikes on civilian and energy infrastructure — the actual threat Saudi Arabia faces, and the clause on which the pact will either bind or not; whether any fourth state accedes; and Indian, Israeli, Iranian and US reactions, none of which had been formally issued at the time of writing. reactions pending

HormuzIran's terms, in writing, are worse than the "reopening" headlines. The plan under review in the Majlis would ban vessels linked to the US, Israel and other "hostile countries" from transiting the Strait, levy fees of up to 7% of cargo value on commercial ships, and fine violators 20% of cargo value. Hostile-designated states would pay compensation before passage. The Trump administration has rejected the plan outright. Separately, Iran–Oman technical talks on inbound and outbound routing have run more than three weeks and have produced broad agreement on the corridors themselves — which is why both Washington and Tehran keep saying a deal is close while describing two different things. Iran also struck what it called "hostile targets" in the Strait after explosions near Qeshm Island.

The gap that matters: routing coordinates are a technical arrangement Tehran can sell domestically as a neighbourly shipping deal. A published toll schedule is a sovereignty claim over an international strait, and it is the thing the US has said it will not accept. TürkiyeMecca in the morning, the framework law in the afternoon

TBMMThe framework law reached committee today. The TBMM Justice Commission convened at 15:30 to take up the 12-article "Terörsüz Türkiye" framework bill, submitted with 360 signatures from AKP, MHP, DEM Party, CHP, HÜDA PAR, some Yeni Yol group members and independents. The plan is a General Assembly vote on Sunday, 9 August.

The substance, and the catch. Organisation leaders are excluded from scope; those convicted of intentional homicide and life sentences are excluded; a monitoring commission is to be established in parliament; the statute of limitations does not run during the deferment period. Crucially, nothing operates until the National Security Council (MGK) verifies that all PKK/KCK-linked structures have dissolved and weapons have been fully surrendered, and that determination is published in the Official Gazette — after which applicants have six months to apply. The law can pass Sunday and still do nothing for months. committee outcome not yet reported at time of writing

ChinaThe export machine did not slow down

Trade dataJuly exports rose 23.9%, beating a 23% median forecast, on AI-infrastructure demand. Total goods trade expanded 19.2% year-on-year to ¥4.66trn (~$686.3bn). The trade surplus narrowed to $112.5bn from $125.62bn in June — but it was the third consecutive month above $100bn, putting China on pace to exceed last year's record $1.19trn annual surplus. Exports of high-tech goods — industrial robots, 3D printers — surged more than 50% year-on-year, against 39% growth in H1.

Read it against the surveys. Last week's official PMI was 49.2 with new orders at a 2023 low, and the private gauge came in at 50.9 against 51.5 expected with export orders contracting. Today's customs data says the opposite. The reconciliation is composition: the AI buildout is pulling a narrow band of high-value manufacturing while the broad domestic economy stays soft. GDP grew 4.7% in H1 but decelerated to 4.3% in Q2, against a 4.5–5% full-year target.

Still on the clock: the US–China tariff truce holding tariffs near 30% expires 10 November 2026. And on chips — the H200 approval was codified into a Commerce rule on 13 January with a 50%-of-US-volume cap, third-party shipment testing and military-use certification; Nvidia's CFO has said the company had yet to generate any revenue from China H200 sales. The constraint is uncertainty, not enforcement.

JapanA record intervention that is already half undone — and a shifted sentence at Hiroshima

FXAn estimated $53bn (¥8.45trn) in one day. Bloomberg's analysis of BOJ accounts puts Thursday's yen defence at likely the largest single-day currency intervention on record, driving the yen up as much as 3.3% intraday — its biggest one-day gain since December 2023 — to a strong point of 155.23 on Monday. By Friday morning the yen was back near 158.45, having surrendered close to half the gain, and traders are openly speculating about a repeat. Context: this follows a $73bn operation begun in late April after the yen breached 160, and confirmed interventions on 30 April, 4 May and 6 May.

The bind. The BOJ held at 1% while upgrading its economic assessment, warning inflation could overshoot 2% and signalling continued normalisation — a hawkish hold. Meanwhile PM Takaichi has reportedly asked Governor Ueda to buy JGBs if needed to cap long-term yields. Buying bonds to suppress yields while selling reserves to support the currency are opposing operations; only one can win, and intervention has never yet fixed a rate differential.

Sources: Bloomberg · Al Jazeera · Bloomberg · Reuters Breakingviews

Nuclear policyThe verb changed at Hiroshima. Attending the 81st anniversary ceremony as prime minister for the first time, Takaichi said Japan "is upholding" the Three Non-Nuclear Principles — not possessing, not producing, not permitting nuclear weapons on Japanese soil. That is a description of the present, not a pledge about the future, and it breaks with Ishiba last year and Kishida before him, both of whom explicitly committed to maintaining them. Asked directly, she said: "It's premature to comment on the final form of the wording as we are in the middle of work to revise the documents before year-end," adding that "it remains unchanged that Japan firmly upholds the three non-nuclear principles in the direction of its policy."

Why the wording is the story. The principles are policy, not law: a cabinet decision altering the language in Japan's national security documents — the revision now under way, due before end-2026 — is legally sufficient to weaken or abandon them. Polling reported by Japan Today put support for maintaining them above two-thirds. Nagasaki marks its 81st on Sunday, 9 August.
